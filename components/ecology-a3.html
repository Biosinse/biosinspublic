01 / Follow the mechanism

From the physical environment to the organism's actual environment

In A2, we examined temperature, light, water, soil, salinity, pH and oxygen. Those measurements describe important parts of a habitat, but they do not fully explain whether an organism can live there. A pond may have suitable temperature and oxygen yet contain a predator that removes a particular prey species. A soil may contain sufficient nutrients, but a plant may fail because its roots are infected or because neighbouring plants intercept the light.

The environment experienced by an organism includes physical and chemical conditions, usable resources, other organisms, and the ways these influences change one another. Ecology asks how these influences affect survival, growth, reproduction, behaviour, abundance and distribution.

A useful question is therefore not simply, 'Is this environment favourable?' Ask instead: favourable for which organism, for which activity or life stage, over what time, and in the presence of which other factors?

The headings A1, A2 and A3 are BioSins teaching labels. This note develops the biotic-environment and organism-response concepts associated with ecological principles; use the official syllabus applicable to your examination session. [1]

1.1 What is an ecological factor?

An ecological factor is an environmental influence relevant to the biological process being studied. It can be measured as a quantity, such as oxygen concentration or competitor density, or described as a treatment, such as predator presence or access to a pollinator.

An influence is not important merely because it exists in the habitat. Its importance depends on a causal connection to the organism. A calcium-rich patch matters differently to a calcifying animal, a plant with sufficient calcium already, and a bird choosing a nesting cavity.

An environmental gradient is a change in a variable across space or time. Gradients may be continuous, such as increasing salinity along an estuary, or organised into distinct treatments in an experiment. Natural gradients usually include several correlated variables.

1.2 The same habitat is not the same environment for every organism

A tree canopy may provide shade for a seedling, nesting space for a bird, food for a caterpillar and a hunting surface for a spider. All occupy the same broad habitat, but their important resources, hazards and response variables differ.

Even a single organism experiences different environments at different scales. A leaf's temperature can differ from the weather-station temperature. A burrowing animal's daytime exposure can differ from conditions above ground. A parasite experiences the host's internal conditions rather than the regional climate directly.

This distinction prevents a common mistake: a regional environmental measurement is not automatically the exposure actually experienced by the organism.

1.3 A connected response, not a list of independent effects

For conceptual purposes, write:

Biological response = f(conditions, resources, biotic interactions; genotype, life stage, previous exposure and time).

This is a framework, not a single universal equation. It reminds us that an observed response has both an environmental context and an organismal context. Two fish exposed to the same oxygen level can differ because of species, body size, acclimation, activity or prior stress.

A2 explained what individual physical factors do. A3 explains how to classify their roles, bring biotic influences into the picture, and recognise responses to combinations. A4 will then examine limitation and tolerance more systematically.

02 / Follow the mechanism

Classifying ecological factors without confusing their roles

2.1 Abiotic and biotic describe different sources of influence

Abiotic factors are physical or chemical features, such as temperature, radiation, water potential, salinity and pH. Biotic factors are influences associated with organisms and their interactions, such as grazing, competition, infection, pollination and social contact.

Ecologists also describe climatic factors, soil-related or edaphic factors, and topographic influences such as aspect and slope. These groupings describe where an influence arises, not a separate law of response. Human activities can alter physical conditions, resource supply and species interactions simultaneously.

These categories do not mean that physical chemistry operates separately from living systems. Photosynthesis changes oxygen and carbon dioxide; roots alter rhizosphere chemistry; vegetation changes wind and temperature. A chemical measurement can therefore describe an abiotic state that has been strongly modified by organisms.

Likewise, 'biotic' does not mean harmful. A neighbouring organism can provide food, shelter, a mating opportunity or protection, as well as impose competition or disease.

2.2 Origin, mechanism and role are separate classifications

Consider a fallen leaf. Its material has a biological origin, but it is no longer a living competitor. It can be detrital food, a source of dissolved chemicals, a physical cover, or a substrate colonised by microbes. Calling it simply 'biotic' or 'abiotic' can hide the mechanism.

A more useful explanation identifies the relevant process: a detritivore consumes leaf material; a seedling experiences reduced light beneath litter; a microbe encounters carbon compounds released during decomposition. Biological origin does not determine whether an influence currently functions as a resource, condition or structural feature.

Soil illustrates this particularly well. Mineral particles, water and chemical conditions interact with living roots, fungi, bacteria and animals. The whole soil system cannot be described accurately as just a nonliving factor.

2.3 A factor can be classified along several independent axes

The same event can occupy several categories. Herbivory is a biotic, interspecific interaction; it directly removes plant tissue and can indirectly change plant competition or nutrient cycling.

2.4 Density dependence is not another name for biotic influence

A density-dependent effect changes in strength with population density, in a specified way and over the range studied. Resource competition or some infection processes can generate such effects. However, a factor being biotic does not prove that its effect must always strengthen as density rises.

Conversely, exposure to an abiotic event can depend on density. Crowded plants may alter humidity or compete for water during drought. A physical driver can therefore have consequences mediated by population density. Detailed population regulation is treated later; here the essential point is to identify the mechanism rather than infer it from a label.

03 / Follow the mechanism

Conditions versus resources: the central distinction

3.1 Conditions describe the setting in which processes operate

An environmental condition is a physical or chemical state that affects functioning without being consumed through the particular use under consideration. Temperature, pH and hydrostatic pressure commonly function as conditions.

An animal does not use up the surrounding temperature by carrying out enzyme reactions. A plant does not consume the numerical pH value as though it were a stock of nutrient. These conditions can be altered, but alteration and consumption are different processes.

Conditions may influence reaction rates, membrane function, water balance, ion transport, chemical speciation or the costs of maintaining internal stability. A value suitable for one process need not maximise every other process.

3.2 Resources are used, occupied or intercepted

A resource is something an organism uses to support maintenance, growth or reproduction, and whose availability to itself or other organisms can be reduced by that use. Examples include food, mineral nutrients, carbon dioxide for photosynthesis, oxygen for aerobic metabolism, intercepted light, usable water and occupied nesting sites.

Consumption need not mean eating, and depletion need not mean destruction of matter. A nesting cavity is still physically present after occupation, but it may no longer be available to another bird. Light intercepted by an upper leaf is less available to a lower leaf. Nitrogen incorporated into biomass is temporarily unavailable in the surrounding dissolved pool.

Resource-based competition theory connects such use to the resource levels that organisms can maintain and the levels needed for persistence. Its predictions require explicit assumptions about supply, use and losses. [2]

3.3 The distinction is functional, not a permanent property of a word

Ask what is being used and what is being measured. Light intensity can describe an exposure condition for an animal's visual behaviour, while absorbed photons provide an energy resource for photosynthesis. Water is consumed or withdrawn as a resource, while the water potential or humidity of the surroundings describes a condition affecting movement and loss.

Oxygen concentration describes an environmental state, while oxygen molecules consumed in aerobic respiration are a resource. Space can be pre-empted as a resource, whereas the temperature or exposure associated with that space is a condition. The labels are useful only when the biological role is specified.

Some teaching definitions reserve 'condition' for abiotic variables. Under that convention, predator presence is classified as a biotic influence, not as an abiotic condition, even though it is part of the organism's overall environmental setting. State the convention when interpreting an examination question.

3.5 Being renewable does not prevent local depletion

A resource can be continuously replenished yet remain limiting. Oxygen can enter a pond from the atmosphere while being consumed faster than it is replaced. Rain can replenish soil water while roots deplete the zone around them. Solar radiation arrives as a flux, but a lower canopy can still receive too little because upper leaves intercept it.

The relevant comparison is therefore rate of supply versus rate of use, together with losses and accessibility. 'Renewable' does not mean 'available without limit at every location and time'.

3.6 Modification does not turn every condition into a resource

A burrowing animal may change soil temperature; a plant may change surrounding pH; microbial respiration may change redox conditions. These effects do not imply that temperature, pH or redox potential has been eaten or incorporated as a resource.

Explain the underlying material or energy transfers. Roots may exchange ions and release compounds that change pH. A canopy may intercept radiation that changes the heat balance. The variable that changes is not always the substance being consumed.

04 / Follow the mechanism

Biotic factors: how other organisms alter performance

4.1 Intraspecific and interspecific influences

Intraspecific interactions occur among members of the same species; interspecific interactions occur between species. Individuals of one species may compete for food, mates or nesting space, but may also cooperate in defence or reproduction.

Different species can compete, consume one another, transmit infection, exchange benefits or modify habitat. The effect depends on abundance, body size, developmental stage, spatial arrangement and environmental context. A species name alone does not specify the interaction strength.

4.2 Interaction signs describe measured consequences

The familiar signs +, -, and 0 represent beneficial, harmful and undetected or negligible effects relative to a stated comparison. Usually the biological interest is fitness or a defensible component such as survival or reproduction.

InteractionEffect on AEffect on BInterpretation
Competition--Each experiences a cost relative to an appropriate comparison
Predation, herbivory or parasitism+-Consumer benefits while the organism used is harmed
Mutualism++Both benefit under the measured conditions
Commensalism+0One benefits; an effect on the other is not detected or is negligible at the scale studied
Amensalism-0One is harmed; an effect on the other is not detected or is negligible

Here A and B identify the first and second participant, respectively. The ordering of the participants matters. 'Predator-prey' is +/-, whereas 'prey-predator' is -/+. A symbol of zero is not proof that no effect could ever be found with another response variable, timescale or experiment. Neutralism is difficult to establish rigorously in an interconnected community.

4.3 Competition: depletion and direct interference

Exploitation competition occurs when organisms reduce a shared resource through use. Two root systems drawing from the same available-water pool can affect one another without physical confrontation. Food or nutrient overlap alone is not sufficient evidence; the shared item must be scarce enough that one user's activity reduces another's performance.

Interference competition includes direct obstruction, aggression, exclusion or other actions that reduce access. A territory holder can exclude a rival from feeding space. Sessile organisms can overgrow or displace neighbours.

The mechanisms can coexist. An organism may both consume food and defend access to it. Intraspecific competition is often substantial because requirements overlap, but it is not automatically stronger than every interspecific interaction.

A classic barnacle-removal study demonstrated that a competitor could restrict part of another species' observed distribution. Its importance is experimental: a field distribution was tested by changing the biological neighbourhood, rather than inferred from co-occurrence alone. [3]

4.4 Consumers, parasites and pathogens

Predators commonly kill prey during consumption; herbivores consume plant or algal material and do not necessarily kill the whole organism in a feeding event. Parasites generally obtain resources through an association with a host, while parasitoid development typically ends in host death. These categories differ in interaction duration and the scale of consumption, although real life histories can be complex.

Pathogens can reduce survival, growth, fertility or competitive ability. Infection depends on exposure, transmission, susceptibility and the environment. Finding a pathogen and a declining host population together is not by itself proof that the pathogen caused the decline.

Food quantity is not the only consumer-related influence. Prey may spend more time hiding or alter their diet in response to risk, sacrificing growth or reproduction even without being killed. Thus the presence of a predator can influence prey through both consumption and non-consumptive responses.

4.5 Mutualists, facilitators and required partners

A pollinator may provide access to reproduction; a symbiotic microorganism may improve nutrient acquisition; a neighbouring plant may reduce damaging exposure. An interaction can be obligatory for one partner yet facultative for the other. Obligate does not mean that every life stage of both partners must always interact.

Facilitation describes a positive effect of one organism on another. It can involve stress reduction or habitat modification without a demonstrated reciprocal benefit. Do not automatically relabel every facilitation example as mutualism.

A benefit can have a cost. A plant may supply carbon to a microbial partner while receiving nutrients. Whether the net outcome remains positive can depend on nutrient supply, partner identity and environmental conditions. Symbiosis describes a close association and is not synonymous with mutualism.

4.6 Detrital pathways and chemical influences

Detritivores ingest dead organic matter, while microbial decomposers release enzymes and transform organic compounds. Their activities influence nutrient availability and oxygen demand. These are biological processes whose effects can appear as chemical changes in the habitat.

Allelopathy concerns effects mediated by chemicals released by organisms, commonly discussed for plants and microbes. An inhibitory chemical effect may contribute to competition, but not every suppression near a plant demonstrates allelopathy. Shade, water depletion, pathogens and altered soil chemistry must be considered as alternatives.

An informative experiment separates chemical exposure from other differences and uses realistic concentrations. Laboratory inhibition at a high extract concentration does not automatically demonstrate a strong field effect.

05 / Follow the mechanism

Direct effects, indirect effects and environmental feedbacks

5.1 Direct and indirect describe pathways

A direct effect operates through the focal pair's immediate interaction. An indirect effect passes through an intermediate organism, resource or environmental change. A predator directly affects herbivores; a resulting reduction in grazing can indirectly benefit plants.

A trophic cascade is an indirect effect transmitted across trophic relationships. The outcome depends on feeding links and alternative pathways, so adding a predator does not guarantee that every plant species increases.

Paine's sea-star-removal work showed how predation could prevent a strong space competitor from monopolising an intertidal assemblage. The broader lesson is that a consumer can indirectly affect organisms it does not consume. [4]

5.2 Density-mediated and trait-mediated effects

A density-mediated indirect effect arises because one participant changes the number of individuals in an intermediate population. Fewer herbivores may consume less vegetation.

A trait-mediated indirect effect arises because the intermediate organisms change behaviour, morphology, physiology or life history. Herbivores may move into different vegetation or reduce feeding when predators are nearby, even if their abundance does not change.

An experiment using predators unable to kill prey can help separate perceived risk from direct mortality, provided the manipulation and handling controls are adequate. An old-field food-chain experiment used this logic to investigate behaviour-mediated effects on plants. [5]

5.3 Apparent competition is not shared-resource competition

Two prey species may reduce one another's abundance by supporting a shared predator or pathogen. An increase in one prey can increase enemy abundance or change enemy behaviour, increasing pressure on the other prey.

This is apparent competition: the negative connection is mediated by a shared enemy, rather than depletion of a shared limiting food resource. Demonstrating it requires evidence for that pathway. Two prey species declining together does not establish apparent competition. [19]

5.4 Ecosystem engineers change the physical setting

Ecosystem engineering involves physical changes that modify the availability of resources or habitat for other organisms. Beaver dams alter water movement; burrowing animals change soil structure; habitat-forming vegetation changes local exposure. [6]

Engineering through an organism's own structures is often called autogenic, while transformation of external material is called allogenic. The categories describe mechanisms, not an organism's permanent rank or importance.

A keystone species has an unusually large community effect relative to its abundance in the relevant setting. It need not be an engineer, and an engineer need not meet a keystone criterion. Likewise, abundant foundation species and keystone species should not be treated as interchangeable labels.

5.5 Feedbacks connect organisms to their own future environment

A negative feedback opposes an initial change: resource depletion may reduce growth and hence reduce further consumption. A positive feedback reinforces an initial change: loss of protective vegetation may increase exposure and make further vegetation loss more likely.

Positive and negative here describe the direction of feedback, not whether the outcome is beneficial or harmful. The feedback must be specified around a particular variable and pathway.

Organisms are therefore both responders and environmental modifiers. A static list of 'abiotic factors' misses the fact that those factors can partly be products of biological activity.

06 / Follow the mechanism

Resource supply, quality and the organism's demand

6.1 Quantity does not equal effective availability

An organism must be able to locate, access, take up and use a resource. Total soil phosphorus does not equal immediately available phosphorus; total prey biomass does not equal edible food for every predator. Size, chemical form, digestibility, protective structures and toxins can change usability.

Resource quality includes composition relative to need. A food supply can provide sufficient carbon or energy but insufficient nitrogen, phosphorus or another essential component. More of the same nutritionally imbalanced food need not remove the constraint.

Changes in temperature, pH or microbial activity can alter resource availability without changing the total amount in the wider system. The mechanism may involve chemical speciation, transport or release from stored material.

6.2 Essential, substitutable and complementary resources

Essential resources meet distinct requirements that cannot fully replace one another. More light cannot supply missing phosphorus atoms. Substitutable resources can replace one another to some extent for a specified requirement, as when a consumer can obtain energy from different foods.

Complementary resources provide a greater benefit in combination than expected from considering each alone under the relevant model, such as foods that supply different missing nutrients. Some resource combinations can instead interfere with use or become harmful at high concentrations.

These categories are organism- and requirement-specific. Two foods may substitute as energy sources while differing in an essential nutrient. 'Both are food' is therefore insufficient to predict complete substitution.

6.3 Supply rate and standing stock are not interchangeable

The standing stock is the amount present at a particular time. The supply rate is the amount entering or becoming available per unit time. A small standing stock can coexist with rapid supply and rapid use, while a large stock can accumulate because it is poorly usable or consumers are inactive.

A snapshot of low dissolved nutrient concentration does not, by itself, identify low nutrient supply. It may reflect rapid biological uptake. Measurements of inputs, transformations and uptake are needed to distinguish these possibilities.

For an illustrative resource balance, write:

dR/dt = S - L - N q(R).

Here R is resource amount per unit area, S is its supply rate per unit area, L is the rate of non-consumer losses per unit area, N is consumer number per unit area, and q(R) is resource consumption per consumer per unit time. All terms on the right must have the same units. This is an accounting framework, not a complete model for every ecosystem.

If supply is 50 mg nutrient m^-2 day^-1, other losses are 10 mg m^-2 day^-1, and 20 consumers m^-2 each use 1 mg day^-1, then dR/dt = 50 - 10 - 20 = 20 mg m^-2 day^-1. Resource stock is increasing at that instant, assuming the specified rates apply.

6.4 Supply and demand both change

A warmer consumer may require more energy while the resource supply remains unchanged. A developing larva may shift to a different food size. A plant entering reproduction may change its allocation demands. Resource limitation is therefore a relationship between available supply and organismal demand, not a fixed property of the habitat alone.

The same supply can support a small inactive organism but not a large active one. Conversely, a large supply of unusable material can fail to meet even a small organism's requirement.

07 / Follow the mechanism

Reading resource responses and the conditions around them

7.1 A saturating resource response

Over a suitable range, increasing a scarce resource often increases uptake or growth, followed by a plateau as other processes constrain the response. One simple formulation is the Monod relationship:

mu(R) = mu_max R / (K_R + R).

Here mu is a specific growth rate, mu_max is its asymptotic maximum under the stated conditions, R is resource concentration, and K_R is the concentration at which mu is half of mu_max. The relationship is a model, originally developed for microbial growth; fitting a saturating curve does not establish every underlying mechanism. [12]

At R = K_R, the fraction is 1/2. At R = 0, the modelled resource-dependent gross growth is zero. At very high R, mu approaches mu_max. K_R has the same concentration units as R, whereas mu and mu_max have inverse-time units.

This simple equation does not include toxicity at excessive supply, changing temperature, multiple resources, storage, variable food quality or all forms of mortality. Do not extrapolate it into those situations without modifying the model.

7.2 Growth must cover losses

For an illustrative population, let the per-capita net growth rate be:

r_net = mu_max R / (K_R + R) - m.

The term m represents the specified per-capita loss rate. At the break-even resource concentration R*, net growth is zero. Solving the equation gives:

R* = m K_R / (mu_max - m), provided mu_max > m.

If mu_max = 1 day^-1, m = 0.2 day^-1 and K_R = 4 mg L^-1, then R* = (0.2 x 4)/(1 - 0.2) = 1 mg L^-1. At this concentration, gross growth just balances the stated losses.

If mu_max is less than or equal to a positive m, this formulation has no finite nonnegative resource level that produces positive net growth. Very abundant resource cannot compensate for every adverse condition or loss process.

Under a restricted, single-resource equilibrium competition model, the species able to persist at the lower R* can reduce the resource below a competitor's requirement. This does not guarantee its success when predators, multiple resources, fluctuating conditions, spatial refuges or other differences matter. [2]

7.3 Conditions can change the entire resource-response curve

Suppose two temperatures give different mu_max values. Even at the same nutrient concentration, the growth rates can differ. If warming increases maintenance loss m or damages uptake, it can change the break-even requirement as well.

Thus 'enough food' cannot be defined independently of conditions and demand. A condition may change acquisition, assimilation, maintenance cost or resource quality. Curves measured at one temperature cannot automatically be applied to another.

7.4 Resource addition and co-limitation

A response to adding one resource shows that the addition mattered under the experiment's conditions. It does not prove that no other resource constrains performance. After one shortage is relieved, a second may become important.

Consider an original teaching dataset: control biomass 10 units; nitrogen alone 12; phosphorus alone 11; both together 25. The combined treatment suggests that considering either addition in isolation misses part of the response. Replication and further dose treatments would help distinguish mechanisms.

Sequential limitation describes a shift in the influential resource after another is supplied. Simultaneous co-limitation involves more than one resource constraining the response over the range considered. Different biochemical and experimental definitions exist, so do not infer a unique mechanism from the word 'co-limitation' alone. [8]

Essential versus substitutable resources can be explored with a two-resource response surface. If each resource is essential, abundant supply of one cannot maintain growth when the other is effectively absent. Detailed limitation laws and their assumptions follow in A4.

08 / Follow the mechanism

How organisms respond: behaviour, physiology and development

8.1 Behaviour changes the exposure actually experienced

An organism can change location, activity time, feeding behaviour or social association. A lizard moving between sun and shade changes heat exposure. A fish shifting depth changes the combination of temperature, oxygen, food and predation risk it encounters.

Taxis is directional movement relative to a stimulus, such as movement towards or away from a light source. Kinesis is a change in movement rate or turning that is not itself directed along the stimulus gradient. Organisms can accumulate in favourable patches through changes in movement even without continuously orienting towards the patch.

Behaviour does not remove physiological limits. A refuge must exist, be detectable and be reachable. Moving may also increase predation risk or reduce access to food, mates or nesting sites.

8.2 Regulation and conformity describe particular variables

A regulator maintains a specified internal variable within a relatively restricted range despite external change, over the range where regulation works. A conformer allows that measured internal variable to change more closely with the external state.

These are not complete labels for every aspect of an organism. An animal may allow body temperature to vary while regulating ions, pH or glucose. An osmoconformer may match total osmotic concentration yet selectively regulate individual ions.

Regulation often requires sensing, feedback, transport and energy expenditure. It has finite capacity. A flat internal-versus-external curve over one interval does not demonstrate unlimited regulation. Conversely, conformity does not mean an absence of all active physiological control.

Endothermy concerns the contribution of metabolic heat to thermal balance; homeothermy concerns relative body-temperature stability. Keep the source of heat separate from the degree of stability, as established in A2.

8.3 Acclimation is a within-lifetime change

Acclimation commonly describes physiological adjustment to a changed environment during an individual's lifetime, often studied under controlled conditions. Acclimatisation commonly refers to adjustment in a natural setting where several factors can change together. Usage varies, so the experimental description is more informative than the label alone.

Possible adjustments include altered enzyme expression, membrane composition, ion transport or metabolic allocation. Such changes may improve performance under the new exposure, but they can be incomplete, costly or beneficial only for certain traits.

A response observed immediately after transfer can differ from the response after several days. Record both the current test condition and the preceding exposure history.

8.4 Phenotypic plasticity is broader than acclimation

Phenotypic plasticity is the capacity of a genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments. It includes behavioural, physiological, morphological and developmental changes. Some are reversible; others depend on a developmental window and persist after the original exposure ends.

Plasticity is not automatically adaptive. A stress-damaged individual also differs phenotypically from an unstressed individual. Demonstrating adaptive plasticity requires evidence that the environmental response improves fitness, relative to relevant alternatives in the relevant setting.

Predator-induced defensive structures in Daphnia pulex provide a mechanistic example of environmentally induced morphology. Chemical information associated with a predator can trigger a developmental defence; the response depends on the organism's developmental and genetic context. This is not evidence that an individual genetically evolves during the exposure. [11]

Population density can also be an inducing influence. In desert locusts, crowding-associated sensory information can induce behavioural gregarisation, with serotonin implicated experimentally in that transition. This illustrates a biotic influence acting through behaviour and physiology. It does not mean every insect responds to crowding in the same way. [20]

8.5 Reaction norms separate environmental response from average differences

A reaction norm describes how a genotype's phenotype changes across environments. On a graph, put the environment on the horizontal axis and the measured phenotype on the vertical axis, with a separate line or curve for each genotype.

  • A changing line shows plasticity for that genotype and trait over the tested range.
  • Parallel separated lines indicate genotype differences and similar environmental responses on that measurement scale.
  • Nonparallel lines indicate that the environmental response differs among genotypes: a genotype-by-environment interaction, or G x E.
  • Crossing lines show a reversal in genotype ranking, but crossing is not required for G x E.
  • A flat line for one trait does not show that the genotype lacks plasticity for every other trait.

A reaction norm alone does not establish that the response is adaptive. The measured trait may not map simply onto fitness. Genetic differences in mean phenotype, plasticity and local fitness advantage are related but distinct questions. [22]

8.6 Plasticity, local adaptation and evolutionary adaptation

Evolutionary adaptation involves inherited population characteristics shaped by evolutionary processes, especially natural selection. An individual's acclimation during one exposure is not the same process as a change in inherited population composition across generations.

A common-garden experiment raises organisms from different origins in a shared setting. Persistent differences can support inherited differentiation, but maternal effects, carry-over effects, age, developmental history and experimental design must be considered.

A reciprocal transplant compares populations in multiple environments, often including their home sites. Evidence for local adaptation is commonly sought when local organisms outperform foreign organisms in their own habitat, using a relevant fitness measure. Simply finding that one population grows better everywhere is not evidence of reciprocal local advantage. [10]

An environmental response can have a genetic basis without being a fixed phenotype. The capacity for plasticity itself can differ genetically and evolve.

09 / Follow the mechanism

Time, exposure history, life stage and trade-offs

9.1 Duration and sequence matter

A brief exposure, a sustained exposure and repeated pulses can produce different outcomes even when their maximum intensity is the same. Recovery periods may allow repair, while repeated events can accumulate damage or change energy reserves.

Exposure order also matters. Previous mild stress can sometimes increase later tolerance, but prior exposure can also leave an organism depleted or injured. Cross-tolerance describes protection extending to another stressor; it must be demonstrated for the conditions studied, not assumed from a generic stress response.

A pulse is a relatively brief perturbation; a press persists; a ramp changes progressively. These descriptions help identify the exposure pattern. They do not independently establish the biological consequence.

9.2 Cues can anticipate conditions

A cue provides information that affects a response. Day length may help time seasonal development before temperature becomes extreme. A predator-derived chemical cue can alter prey behaviour before an attack occurs.

A cue is not necessarily a resource or a damaging stressor. Its usefulness depends on its reliability and on the costs of responding. False alarms may reduce feeding or reproduction, whereas failing to respond to a real threat can increase mortality.

This distinction also separates proximate information, which triggers a response, from a possible fitness consequence, which concerns its effects on survival or reproduction.

9.3 Dormancy and reduced activity

Organisms may suspend development, reduce activity, form resistant stages or enter physiological states that bridge unfavourable periods. These strategies retain costs and limits; dormant organisms still face damage, depletion or failure to resume successfully.

Diapause is a regulated developmental or reproductive delay, often initiated through predictive cues and not simply terminated by an immediate improvement in conditions. Quiescence more directly follows adverse current conditions and can end when those conditions improve, although details vary among organisms. [18]

Torpor involves a regulated reduction in metabolic rate and often body temperature in suitable animals. Hibernation and aestivation describe forms of prolonged seasonal or environmentally associated inactivity in particular taxa; do not treat every dormant plant, cyst or resting egg as an example of animal torpor.

9.4 Life stages can have different requirements

Eggs, larvae, juveniles and adults may differ in food, exposure, mobility and physiological capacity. A habitat supporting adult survival may fail to support reproduction or juvenile recruitment.

An individual can also shift from one ecological role to another during development. A larva and adult may use different food resources or occupy different habitats. Species persistence requires a workable life cycle, not merely the short-term survival of one stage.

9.5 Allocation produces trade-offs

Resources devoted to defence, repair, ion regulation or heat balance are not simultaneously available for every other function. A plant reducing water loss by closing stomata may also reduce carbon uptake; prey hiding from a predator may lose feeding opportunities.

Trade-offs need not appear as a fixed negative correlation in every dataset. Individuals with greater total resources can sometimes invest more in several functions at once. To infer an allocation trade-off, consider variation in acquisition as well as allocation.

Survival, growth and reproduction are therefore different response variables. A treatment that improves immediate survival can still reduce lifetime reproductive success, and the optimum for one trait need not maximise the others.

Interactions between factors: what nonadditivity means

10.1 Two influences can act together without a statistical interaction

If temperature and salinity both affect growth, they are both relevant factors. A statistical interaction asks a further question: does the effect of one depend on the level of the other, on the response scale being analysed?

Suppose salinity reduces growth by 2 units at both tested temperatures. On the raw growth scale, its effect is additive with temperature. If its effect is -2 at one temperature and -7 at another, the effect depends on temperature and the treatment means show nonadditivity.

Always identify the response variable, its units, the treatment baseline and the comparison model. The mere presence of two factors is not evidence of interaction.

10.2 The four treatments in a two-factor experiment

For two factors A and B, each at two levels, use:

  • Y00: neither treatment added, or both at reference levels.
  • Y10: A applied, B at reference level.
  • Y01: B applied, A at reference level.
  • Y11: A and B both applied.

A complete two-level design with k factors has 2^k treatment combinations, before replication. For two factors there are four combinations; for three factors there are eight. Replication multiplies the number of independent experimental units required. [14]

For an additive null model on the raw response scale:

Expected Y11 = Y10 + Y01 - Y00.

The corresponding difference-in-differences is:

I = Y11 - Y10 - Y01 + Y00.

Equivalently, I is the effect of A when B is present minus the effect of A when B is absent. If I = 0, the four means are additive on this scale. A nonzero estimate must still be evaluated with replication and uncertainty before a reliable population-level interaction is claimed.

A corresponding model is Y = b0 + bA A + bB B + bAB A B + error, with A and B coded 0 or 1. Here b0 is the reference mean, bA and bB are effects when the other factor is at its reference level, and bAB is the interaction contrast I. The effect of A at B = 1 becomes bA + bAB. With different coding, the numerical coefficients change, so always check the coding scheme.

10.3 Worked example: warming and low oxygen

The following values are original teaching data, not a verified past examination question. Let the response be growth in arbitrary units after the same exposure period:

Temperature treatmentAdequate oxygenLow oxygen
Reference temperature10080
Warmer temperature9050

Warming reduces growth by 10 units at adequate oxygen but by 30 units at low oxygen. The additive expectation for the combined treatment is 90 + 80 - 100 = 70, whereas the observed mean is 50.

Thus I = 50 - 90 - 80 + 100 = -20 units. The combined reduction is 20 units greater than the additive prediction. For two individually harmful treatments and a growth response, this can be called a synergistic negative effect relative to that additive null model.

The result does not, by itself, identify the mechanism. Oxygen supply, respiratory demand, feeding or other processes could contribute. Replication and additional measurements are necessary.

10.4 Additive, synergistic and antagonistic effects

With the same reference value 100, A alone 90 and B alone 80, the additive combined expectation is 70.

Observed combined responseComparison with the additive predictionInterpretation for these harmful treatments
70Matches predictionAdditive on this scale
50More damage than predictedSynergistic negative effect
85Less damage than predictedAntagonistic negative effect or partial buffering

Antagonism does not imply that neither factor is harmful, or that the combined treatment must improve the organism above its control. Likewise, synergy is not simply 'two factors together are worse than either alone'. A combined value of 75 is worse than either single treatment here, but is less harmful than the additive prediction of 70.

When individual factors act in opposite directions, simple verbal labels can become ambiguous. State the observed values, expected value, direction and null model. Multiple-stressor research explicitly distinguishes these issues. [9]

10.5 The null model and response scale change the answer

For positive responses with a nonzero baseline, a multiplicative reference can be:

Expected Y11 = (Y10 x Y01) / Y00.

If Y00 = 100, Y10 = 80 and Y01 = 80, the raw additive expectation is 60, but the multiplicative expectation is 64. A combined observation of 64 therefore matches the multiplicative prediction while differing from the additive one.

For survival probability, independence of two additional mortality processes is sometimes represented by multiplying their relative survival fractions. This is a particular biological and statistical null hypothesis, not an automatic rule for every probability or mortality dataset. A baseline mortality, bounded outcomes or changing susceptibility may require a different model.

Log transformation converts a multiplicative relationship among positive values into an additive relationship. Consequently, the presence and interpretation of an interaction can depend on scale. A raw-scale interaction is not automatically a log-scale interaction, or vice versa.

10.6 Interaction plots and main effects

An interaction plot puts one factor on the horizontal axis and draws a separate response line for each level of the second factor. Nonparallel lines in the treatment means suggest an interaction on that plotted scale. Crossing is a strong visual example but is not necessary.

A main effect averages over levels of another factor. Such an average can conceal a large benefit in one setting and a large cost in another. Inspect the separate effects before describing a single overall treatment response.

Error bars must be interpreted according to what they represent: standard deviation, standard error or a confidence interval. Visual separation alone is not a universal significance test. A suitable factorial model is needed for inference.

Mechanisms that connect environmental factors

11.1 Temperature, humidity and plant water balance

At a given relative humidity, warmer air commonly has a larger vapour-pressure deficit because saturation vapour pressure increases with temperature. Thus holding relative humidity constant while changing temperature does not hold atmospheric drying demand constant. [15]

A leaf's water loss also depends on leaf temperature, stomata, wind and the boundary layer. If water supply cannot meet demand, stomatal closure can conserve water while reducing carbon dioxide entry. A treatment described as 'temperature only' may therefore change several physiological influences.

The lesson is to measure the quantity relevant to the mechanism. A nominally controlled variable is not always the biologically important exposure.

11.2 Temperature, oxygen and respiration

Warmer water has a lower equilibrium oxygen solubility under otherwise comparable conditions. At the same time, warming can increase respiratory demand over a suitable physiological range. Stratification can reduce replenishment of oxygen at depth. These pathways can combine to reduce suitable habitat. [16]

The statement does not mean that every warmed pond becomes hypoxic or that oxygen universally sets every organism's thermal limit. Mixing, primary production, community composition, pressure, salinity and acclimation affect the outcome.

Distinguish oxygen concentration, saturation percentage, partial pressure and the rate of delivery to tissues. The same concentration can represent different percent saturation at different temperatures.

11.3 pH and temperature change chemical exposure

A change in pH can alter the chemical form of a substance and hence its uptake or toxicity. Total ammonia includes un-ionised ammonia and ammonium; their relative proportions depend on pH and temperature. The un-ionised fraction generally increases as pH rises, and it is usually the more toxic form for fish. [21]

Therefore equal total-ammonia measurements do not necessarily imply equal exposure to un-ionised ammonia. A combined effect can arise through a chemical change before any additional biological response occurs.

This is a useful general pattern: one factor can change the effective dose of another. Do not assume that the concentration entered into an experiment is identical to the biologically active exposure throughout it.

11.4 Neighbours can compete and facilitate at the same time

A shrub can intercept light and withdraw water while also reducing temperature or wind exposure beneath its canopy. The net effect on a seedling depends on which gains and losses matter in that setting.

The stress-gradient hypothesis proposes that the relative importance of facilitation can increase in stressful environments in some systems. Salt-marsh experiments demonstrated positive associations associated with buffering physical stress. This is a context-dependent hypothesis, not a rule that competition always disappears at high stress. [7]

If neighbours intensify an already severe resource shortage, competition may remain strong. At an extreme beyond the facilitator's own capacity, facilitation may also fail. Species, life stage, density and the kind of stress all matter.

11.5 Abiotic change can alter a mutualism

Coral bleaching links physical exposure to a biological association. Temperature stress, together with factors such as high irradiance, can disrupt the association between corals and their photosynthetic symbionts. A bleached coral is not necessarily already dead, but prolonged loss of symbionts can reduce its energy supply and increase mortality risk. [17]

The example connects an abiotic condition, a biotic partner and a resource pathway. The biological consequence cannot be fully described by listing water temperature alone.

11.6 Enrichment can create a later shortage

Nutrient addition can initially stimulate production. More organic matter can subsequently increase respiration and decomposition, creating oxygen demand. Shading by dense growth and restricted mixing can add further effects.

Thus one resource increase can create a different shortage through biological feedbacks. The first measured response may differ from the later response. Sampling only immediately after enrichment can miss the delayed pathway.

How to test ecological-factor explanations

12.1 Start with the response variable

State exactly what is measured: survival over seven days, growth per day, oxygen consumption, seed production, feeding time or population change. Record whether values are individual, per unit biomass, per unit area or whole-system totals.

A larger plant population can have greater total respiration without a higher respiration rate per individual. A treatment can increase percentage survival while reducing total survivors if the starting populations differ. Units and denominators are part of the explanation.

12.2 Observation generates hypotheses; manipulation tests pathways

A species occurring only in shaded sites may be limited by heat, water loss, a competitor or some correlated condition. Distribution alone rarely identifies the causal mechanism.

Useful manipulations include resource addition, competitor removal, predator exclusion, controlled exposure, transplantation and cue presentation. Each changes a particular part of the proposed pathway and should be matched to a prediction.

If removing a neighbour improves seedling growth, competition is one candidate explanation. But removal may also change shade, litter, soil disturbance or herbivore access. Separate these possibilities rather than treating the manipulation's label as proof of its mechanism.

12.3 Use independent replication and appropriate controls

The experimental unit is the unit independently assigned to a treatment. If an entire tank receives one temperature treatment, fish within that tank are subsamples, not independent temperature replicates. Ten fish in one heated tank and ten in one control tank do not provide ten independently treated tanks per condition.

Pseudoreplication occurs when non-independent observations are treated as independent replicates for an inference that requires independent treatment units. Measure many organisms when useful, but analyse the treatment at the correct level. [13]

Random assignment helps prevent treatment from being systematically confounded with other differences. Blocking can account for a known gradient, such as slope position. Handling controls or partial-cage controls help reveal effects of the manipulation itself.

12.4 Vary the factors needed to test the claim

A one-factor experiment cannot directly establish how that factor interacts with another unvaried factor. A factorial design includes their combinations. Several exposure levels help reveal curvature or thresholds that a two-level design cannot resolve.

Record actual treatment conditions. Heaters can influence oxygen; cages can change flow or shading; nutrient additions can change ionic composition. An apparently simple treatment may have additional effects.

12.5 Match the timescale and the life stage

Include an appropriate acclimation period when the question concerns adjusted physiology, or deliberately measure immediate responses when those are the question. Standardise prior food, age and exposure history where relevant.

Survival at the end of a short experiment does not demonstrate successful reproduction or persistence over generations. Likewise, a delayed effect does not prove that the earlier exposure was harmless.

A well-supported explanation connects treatment, measured exposure, mechanism and response, while recognising the limits of the design.

Worked applications and graph interpretation

All datasets in this section are original BioSins teaching examples, not verified past examination questions.

13.1 A shelter is a resource even when it is not eaten

A population of cavity-nesting birds has abundant food but few suitable nest holes. Adding usable cavities increases the number of successful nests, while adding food has little effect in the same experiment.

The result supports a shortage of suitable nesting opportunity under those conditions. The cavities function as a resource because their occupation reduces access for other birds. It does not follow that food can never become limiting, or that every cavity is equivalent in safety and suitability.

13.2 Resource, condition and cue in one scenario

A plant receives less light beneath a neighbour. Its leaf temperature also falls, and its red:far-red light ratio changes.

  • Reduced absorbed photosynthetic radiation changes an energy resource.
  • Changed leaf temperature changes a physical condition.
  • Changed spectral ratio can provide information about neighbouring vegetation.

A single neighbour can therefore change resource supply, conditions and cues simultaneously. To identify which causes an observed elongation or growth response, manipulate these components separately or use a suitable combined design.

13.3 Calculating a resource-dependent response

Let mu_max = 0.8 day^-1, K_R = 2 mg L^-1 and R = 6 mg L^-1. The saturating model gives:

mu = 0.8 x 6/(2 + 6) = 0.6 day^-1.

With a loss rate m = 0.2 day^-1, net growth is 0.4 day^-1. The break-even requirement is:

R* = (0.2 x 2)/(0.8 - 0.2) = approximately 0.667 mg L^-1.

These are outputs of the specified model. The calculation assumes the resource is the relevant limiting input, conditions and model parameters remain fixed, and the loss term is represented correctly. It does not determine the outcome of a natural multi-species community by itself.

13.4 A context-dependent neighbour effect

Seedling biomass is measured in a replicated neighbour-removal experiment:

Water treatmentNeighbour absentNeighbour present
Well-watered2016
Dry812

Under well-watered conditions the neighbour effect is 16 - 20 = -4. Under dry conditions it is 12 - 8 = +4. The sign changes, so the neighbour's net effect depends on water treatment.

A plausible hypothesis is that competition dominates in the first setting while protection from exposure outweighs costs in the second. However, biomass means alone do not identify the protection mechanism. Measurements of water potential, temperature, light and herbivore access would test competing explanations.

The lines would cross in an interaction plot. This illustrates an interaction between water treatment and biological neighbourhood, not proof that every species facilitates neighbours during drought.

13.5 Reading genotype-by-environment responses

Consider the same two genotypes tested in two environments:

GenotypeEnvironment 1Environment 2
G148
G266

G1 changes by +4 units, while G2 does not change in this measured trait. The lines are nonparallel, and the ranking reverses. The data illustrate plasticity in G1 and a genotype-by-environment interaction on this scale.

They do not tell us whether the plasticity is adaptive unless the trait's relationship to fitness is established. If the response were an inappropriate stress marker rather than fitness, a larger number would not automatically mean better performance.

For comparison, G1 values 4 and 8 together with G2 values 6 and 10 would give parallel responses: both change by +4, with a constant genotype difference of 2 units.

13.6 Correlated environmental variables do not reveal the causal factor

A survey finds fewer fish in warmer, nutrient-rich, slow-moving water. Temperature, oxygen, food, toxic chemicals, pathogens and habitat structure may vary together.

A valid next step is to measure candidate exposures and test predictions, such as crossing temperature and oxygen treatments under controlled food conditions. A simple temperature-abundance correlation cannot establish that temperature alone caused the field pattern.

Connecting A3 to A4: when does an influence become limiting?

We can now distinguish a condition from a resource, identify a biotic pathway, recognise several kinds of organism response, and ask how one factor changes the effect of another.

The next question is more specific: which influence prevents better performance or persistence under the conditions actually present? An organism may have abundant energy but insufficient nitrogen, suitable temperature but damaging oxygen shortage, or adequate resources but a condition outside its functional range.

In A4: Limiting Factors and Tolerance, we use this foundation to examine Liebig's law of the minimum, Shelford's law of tolerance, deficiency and excess, response curves, and the evidence needed to identify the factor that constrains the response. A5 then develops ecological adaptations in greater detail.

The connecting idea is that an organism responds to a combination of conditions, usable resources and relationships, through its particular physiology, behaviour, developmental state and history. Listing the factors is the beginning; explaining their roles and their interactions is the ecological reasoning.
