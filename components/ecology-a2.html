01 / Environment

From ecological organisation to environmental conditions

In A1, we followed a pond from one organism to an interacting ecosystem. We now ask a more specific question: what must that environment provide, and which physical and chemical conditions make life possible there?

A fish may have food and still fail to survive if oxygen is too low. A plant may receive abundant sunlight but grow poorly because its roots cannot obtain water. A wet soil may contain plenty of water yet too little oxygen for normal root function.

The physical environment therefore acts through mechanisms. Temperature affects reaction rates, light supplies energy and timing information, water moves along energy gradients, and chemical conditions influence the availability and toxicity of substances.

The physical-environment topic appears within the ecological-principles syllabus. The organisation and subsection numbers used here are BioSins teaching labels. This note develops the mechanisms, comparisons and quantitative reasoning behind the seven factors. [1]

1.1 Physical and chemical factors belong together

In ecology, the abiotic environment includes both physical variables, such as temperature and radiation, and chemical variables, such as salinity, pH and oxygen availability. Soil combines mineral particles, organic matter, water, air and organisms; calling its properties abiotic does not make the whole soil a nonliving system.

1.2 The conditions an organism experiences may differ from regional averages

Weather describes short-term atmospheric conditions. Climate describes their long-term statistical patterns, including seasonality and variability. Microclimate describes conditions over the small area actually occupied by an organism: beneath a leaf, inside a burrow, at the soil surface or within a canopy.

A weather-station temperature is not automatically an animal's body temperature or a leaf's temperature. Radiation, wind, evaporation, contact with surfaces and behaviour all matter. Even neighbouring microsites can provide different opportunities for survival. [2]

For every factor, ask four questions: what is measured; how does it affect the organism; how does it vary; and what changes when it interacts with another factor?

02 / Environment

Temperature: the rate and heat-balance problem

2.1 Temperature is not the same as heat

Temperature describes thermal state; heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. An organism exchanges heat with its surroundings and may also generate substantial metabolic heat.

Environmental temperature varies with latitude, altitude, season, time of day, depth, exposure and local topography. Water's high heat capacity generally buffers temperature fluctuations compared with exposed land, although shallow water can warm rapidly. Soil, shade and burrows can also buffer extremes.

The biologically relevant temperature is the temperature of the tissue or process being studied. Air temperature is often only a predictor of it.

2.2 Why temperature affects biological processes

Within a suitable range, warming commonly accelerates biochemical reactions and physiological rates. Temperature also changes membrane properties, diffusion, viscosity, protein stability and the balance between energy supply and demand.

At low temperatures, slow reactions and reduced membrane fluidity can limit performance. Freezing can additionally cause ice damage and cellular dehydration. At excessive temperatures, protein damage, membrane disruption, water loss and failure of coordinated physiology can reduce performance.

Consequently, an increase in temperature does not improve every biological process indefinitely. Growth, reproduction, locomotion and survival can have different temperature-response curves.

2.3 Reading a thermal performance curve

A thermal performance curve plots a measured performance against temperature. Many ectotherm performance curves rise towards an optimum and then decline sharply at higher temperatures. [2]

  • Optimum temperature: the temperature giving the highest value of the particular performance measured under the stated conditions.
  • Thermal breadth: the range over which a specified level of performance is maintained; the criterion must be defined.
  • Critical thermal minimum and maximum: experimentally measured limits of a specified function, such as coordinated movement. They are not automatically lethal temperatures.
  • Lethal temperature: a temperature causing death under a stated exposure protocol. Duration and rate of temperature change matter.

Do not assume that an optimum for short-term swimming is also the optimum for lifetime reproduction. Acclimation history, life stage, hydration, oxygen supply and experimental protocol can change the measured response.

Eurythermal organisms tolerate a relatively broad temperature range; stenothermal organisms tolerate a relatively narrow range. A narrow range can occur around a cold or a warm optimum.

2.4 Q10: measuring temperature sensitivity

Q10 describes the factor by which a positive rate changes for a 10-degree Celsius increase over a specified interval:

Q10 = (R2 / R1)^[10 / (T2 - T1)]

R1 and R2 are rates measured in the same units at temperatures T1 and T2. Temperature differences have the same numerical value in Celsius and kelvin.

If Q10 can be treated as constant over the interval:

R2 = R1 x Q10^[(T2 - T1) / 10]

A rate of 4 units at 15 C and 10 units at 25 C gives Q10 = 10/4 = 2.5. For a 20-degree increase, the predicted multiplier would be 2.5 squared, or 6.25, only if the same Q10 remains applicable.

Q10 values around 2 to 3 are useful examples for many uncompensated biological rates, not universal laws. A process can show a different value, and extrapolation through damaging temperatures is invalid. Temperature-compensated circadian timing illustrates why every biological process need not speed up like an ordinary metabolic reaction. [3]

2.5 Arrhenius reasoning

For a reaction over a suitable temperature range:

k = A x exp[-Ea / (R x T)]

Here k is a rate constant, A a pre-exponential factor, Ea activation energy, R the gas constant and T absolute temperature in kelvin.

A plot of ln(k) against 1/T has slope -Ea/R when this model applies. For two temperatures:

ln(k2/k1) = (Ea/R) x (1/T1 - 1/T2)

Use kelvin in this equation. A whole organism's performance eventually departs from a simple Arrhenius increase because multiple processes, resource limitations and thermal injury become important.

2.6 Heat source and body-temperature stability are different classifications

ClassificationWhat it describesMain meaning
EndothermySource of heatMetabolic heat makes a major contribution to maintaining body temperature
EctothermySource of heatHeat exchange with the environment makes the major contribution
HomeothermyStability of temperatureBody temperature remains relatively stable over the conditions considered
PoikilothermyVariability of temperatureBody temperature varies appreciably over the conditions considered
HeterothermyVariation in regulationTemperature differs across times or body regions, or between physiological states

Endothermy and homeothermy are not synonyms. An ectotherm in a thermally stable environment can have a relatively stable body temperature. An endothermic mammal can allow a substantial temperature decline during torpor. Many animals also regulate particular body regions differently. [4]

The labels cold-blooded and warm-blooded are unreliable: a sun-exposed ectotherm can be warmer than a shaded endotherm.

2.7 How organisms gain and lose heat

  • Radiation: exchange of electromagnetic energy; solar absorption can heat a body, while long-wave radiation can contribute to heat loss or gain.
  • Conduction: heat transfer through direct contact, such as with warm rock or cool soil.
  • Convection: heat exchange with moving air or water.
  • Evaporation: heat removal when water evaporates; its effectiveness depends on the vapour-pressure gradient and other conditions.

An organism's heat storage reflects metabolic heat plus environmental heat gains minus heat losses. The direction of conduction and convection depends on the temperature gradient; wind is not automatically a cooling influence when the surrounding air is hotter than the body.

An animal can alter exposure by basking, entering shade, changing posture or using a burrow. Endotherms can also vary heat production, insulation, blood flow and evaporative cooling. Regulation has energetic or water costs.

2.8 Thermoneutral zone and the cost of endothermy

For a resting endotherm under defined conditions, the thermoneutral zone is the range of ambient temperatures over which metabolic expenditure can remain near its minimum without additional regulatory heat production or increased evaporative-cooling expenditure.

Below the lower critical temperature, heat production typically increases. Above the upper critical temperature, active cooling and other responses can increase costs. A graph of metabolic rate against ambient temperature therefore differs from a graph of an ectotherm's temperature-dependent reaction rate.

Body size matters because surface area scales approximately with length squared, while volume scales with length cubed. Smaller similar-shaped bodies have more surface area per unit volume and tend to exchange heat more rapidly per unit mass.

Bergmann's rule describes a tendency towards larger body size in colder environments among related endotherms. Allen's rule describes a tendency towards shorter appendages in colder environments. These are ecological generalisations with exceptions, not universal predictions for all organisms.

2.9 Duration, variability and developmental timing

The same mean temperature can conceal very different extremes. Because temperature-response curves are nonlinear, average performance under fluctuating temperatures need not equal performance at the average temperature. The direction of the difference depends on the curve and the temperatures experienced.

Development can sometimes be predicted using growing degree days (GDD) or thermal time. In a simple daily-mean model:

GDD = max[0, (Tmax + Tmin)/2 - Tbase]

With Tmax = 30 C, Tmin = 14 C and Tbase = 10 C, the day contributes 12 degree-days. Sum daily values to obtain accumulated thermal time. The base temperature and any upper-temperature cutoff are organism- and model-specific; the simple formula is not valid for every temperature regime. [5]

Temperature can also act as a developmental signal. Vernalisation is the promotion of flowering by a prolonged cold treatment in responsive plants. Chilling requirements for release from bud dormancy and the timing of germination are related seasonal topics but are not identical to vernalisation. Cold exposure, photoperiod and developmental stage can interact.

2.10 Temperature structures aquatic habitats

In many seasonally stratifying lakes, the warm upper epilimnion lies above a transition region, the metalimnion, and a cooler hypolimnion. The thermocline is the region or depth of strongest temperature change within that transition.

Density differences resist vertical mixing. Deep water can therefore lose oxygen through respiration and decomposition while remaining poorly replenished from the surface. Clear, nutrient-poor lakes may retain deep oxygen; deep-water anoxia is not inevitable.

Freshwater is densest near 4 C. In lakes that freeze, cold water and ice can remain above denser, somewhat warmer water. Seasonal cooling and wind can promote turnover when density differences weaken. A lake with spring and autumn mixing is dimictic, but shallow, tropical, saline and other lakes may follow different mixing regimes. [6]

Connection: temperature changes the rates of life, but the energy supporting much of that life arrives as light. We must distinguish light's role as an energy supply from its role as an environmental signal.

03 / Environment

Light: energy, information and depth

3.1 Three properties must be separated

Light quantity describes how much radiation or how many photons reach a surface. Light quality describes the wavelength composition. Light duration, or photoperiod, describes the timing and length of light and dark periods.

Two habitats can have the same daily light quantity but different photoperiods. They can also have the same photoperiod but very different intensities. A shaded seedling and an exposed seedling may experience different spectral composition as well as different photon supply.

3.2 Radiation bands and biological effects

The traditional photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) waveband is 400 to 700 nm. It is a measurement convention, not the entire solar spectrum and not a claim that other wavelengths have no biological effects. [7]

Ultraviolet radiation has shorter wavelengths than visible light and can damage DNA and other cellular components. Infrared radiation has longer wavelengths and contributes to heat exchange. Photosynthetic pigments absorb different visible wavelengths with different efficiencies.

Photon energy follows E = h x c / wavelength. A shorter-wavelength photon carries more energy than a longer-wavelength photon. This does not mean that the shortest available wavelength always produces the greatest photosynthetic return: absorption, photochemistry and damage also matter.

An absorption spectrum describes the wavelengths absorbed by a pigment or preparation. An action spectrum describes the biological response at different wavelengths. They are related but are not identical measurements.

3.3 Measuring light correctly

Irradiance, commonly expressed in W m^-2, measures radiant power received per unit area. Photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) measures photons in the specified photosynthetic waveband reaching unit area per unit time, commonly in micromoles of photons m^-2 s^-1.

Lux is weighted for human visual sensitivity. Equal lux values from different spectra need not provide equal PPFD or equal photosynthetic effects.

The daily light integral (DLI) is the daily total of photosynthetic photons received per unit area. For constant PPFD:

DLI = PPFD x light duration in seconds / 1,000,000

At 200 micromoles m^-2 s^-1 for 16 hours, DLI = 200 x 16 x 3,600 / 1,000,000 = 11.52 mol m^-2 day^-1. If PPFD varies, add or integrate photon supply across time rather than using one instantaneous reading. [7]

3.4 Photosynthesis versus light: compensation and saturation

In a simplified carbon-balance model, net photosynthesis = gross carbon fixation - respiratory carbon losses. Keep the definitions and measurement conditions consistent when interpreting data.

In darkness, photosynthetic carbon fixation stops while respiration can continue, so net carbon exchange is negative. As light increases, photosynthesis rises.

  • At the light compensation point, photosynthetic carbon gain equals respiratory carbon loss, giving zero net carbon gain under the measurement conditions.
  • Below compensation, carbon loss exceeds gain.
  • Above compensation, net carbon gain is positive until other constraints become important.
  • Near the light saturation region, additional light produces little further increase in photosynthesis because some other component of the process limits the response.
  • At excessive light, photoinhibition can reduce photosynthetic performance through protective downregulation, damage or both.

Compensation is therefore a zero-balance point, whereas saturation is a flattening of the response at high light. They are not interchangeable. Increasing temperature can raise respiratory costs and change the compensation point. [8]

3.5 Sun leaves and shade leaves

The following are common tendencies, especially when photosynthesis is compared per unit leaf area. Species and growth conditions can modify them.

FeatureSun-acclimated leavesShade-acclimated leaves
Leaf constructionOften thicker, with more tissue per unit areaOften thinner, with greater area per unit dry mass
Light compensation pointOften higherOften lower
Light saturationOften reached at higher irradianceOften reached at lower irradiance
Maximum photosynthesis per areaOften higherOften lower
Low-light functioningHigher construction and maintenance costs can matterEfficient light capture and lower costs support persistence in shade

A shade leaf can perform better at low light without having the higher maximum rate. Chlorophyll content depends on whether it is expressed per leaf area, dry mass or whole leaf; do not compare values without checking the basis.

Shade-acclimated leaves often invest relatively more in light-harvesting antennae and chlorophyll b, giving a lower chlorophyll a:b ratio. This is a common acclimation tendency rather than a universal fixed ratio for every plant.

Leaf responses also reveal plasticity. Differences between sun and shade leaves do not, by themselves, prove that two genetically distinct ecotypes are present.

3.6 Light quality and the shade signal

Leaves absorb much of the red light that reaches them but transmit or reflect relatively more far-red light. A low red:far-red ratio can therefore signal nearby vegetation before severe shading occurs.

Phytochrome-mediated shade avoidance can include stem or internode elongation and altered allocation. Shade avoidance differs from shade tolerance: the first can help escape competition for light, while the second supports functioning under low light. [10]

The phytochrome system includes a red-absorbing Pr form and a far-red-absorbing Pfr form. Red light near 660 nm favours conversion of Pr to Pfr; far-red light near 730 nm favours conversion back. Pfr often initiates the classical responses discussed in introductory experiments. [35]

Light can also control germination in photoblastic seeds. In classic responsive lettuce-seed experiments, red light promotes germination and a subsequent far-red pulse can reverse the effect. Species, seed condition and treatment history matter. Blue-light receptors also contribute to environmental responses: phototropins participate in phototropism and stomatal opening, while cryptochromes contribute to light signalling and clock-related responses.

3.7 Photoperiodism and the importance of night

Photoperiodism is a biological response to the relative duration or timing of light and darkness. In plants it can influence flowering, dormancy and other developmental events. In animals, day length can help time seasonal breeding, migration or diapause.

  • Short-day plants commonly flower when the uninterrupted night exceeds a critical duration; they are often better described as long-night plants.
  • Long-day plants commonly flower when the night is shorter than a critical duration.
  • Day-neutral plants do not require a particular photoperiod for flowering, although light can still affect growth and other processes.

Critical durations are species- or cultivar-specific. Short day does not universally mean less than 12 hours, and long day does not universally mean more than 12 hours. Obligate and facultative responses also differ. [9]

In classic night-break experiments, a brief red-light interruption of a long night can inhibit flowering in a short-day plant and promote it in a responsive long-day plant. An immediately following far-red pulse can reverse the red effect in suitable phytochrome experiments. The last effective pulse is useful reasoning for these classic reversible treatments; it is not a universal rule for every light response.

Photoperiod is a timing signal, not a direct measure of daily photosynthetic energy. Circadian clocks contribute to the interpretation of the light-dark cycle.

3.8 Light in water

Light decreases with depth because of absorption and scattering. In clear oceanic water, blue wavelengths generally penetrate farther than red wavelengths. Dissolved substances, suspended particles and organisms alter this pattern; coastal and inland waters need not have the same spectral transmission as clear ocean water. [11]

A simple attenuation model is:

I(z) = I0 x exp(-k x z)

I0 is irradiance at the reference surface, I(z) irradiance at depth z, and k an attenuation coefficient with units inverse to those used for depth. The model assumes an appropriate effective, constant k over the interval.

Taking natural logarithms gives ln[I(z)/I0] = -k x z. Greater k means more rapid attenuation and shallower light penetration.

Compensation depth is the depth at which the relevant organism's photosynthesis balances respiration under the stated conditions. The commonly used 1%-of-surface-light depth is an operational estimate for the euphotic zone, not a universal biological compensation threshold.

Critical depth in the classic phytoplankton model is different: production integrated over a mixed water column balances integrated respiratory losses. The distinction matters when mixing carries cells above and below their compensation depth.

Connection: light can support carbon gain, but leaves must exchange gases and maintain hydration. Water availability determines whether an organism can use the light it receives.

04 / Environment

Water: availability, movement and water balance

4.1 Presence is not the same as availability

Water is a solvent, reactant, transport medium and thermal buffer. Its availability influences enzyme function, cell volume, plant turgor, nutrient transport and the maintenance of body fluids.

Hydrogen bonding contributes to cohesion between water molecules and adhesion to surfaces, as well as water's thermal properties. Surface tension creates a physical interface used by some small organisms. These properties connect water's molecular behaviour to ecological consequences.

Annual rainfall alone does not describe the water environment. Timing, intensity, runoff, infiltration, storage, snowmelt, evaporation, rooting depth and soil properties all affect access to water. Two places receiving the same annual rainfall can differ greatly in drought exposure.

Water can also be present but difficult to obtain because it is frozen, held tightly by soil or associated with high dissolved-salt concentrations. Waterlogging presents another problem: much of the pore space is water-filled, restricting oxygen supply.

4.2 Water potential explains the direction of movement

Water potential, written here as Psi, expresses the energy state of water relative to a reference state and is commonly measured in megapascals, MPa. Pure water at the reference temperature, pressure and elevation is assigned zero water potential.

A useful decomposition is:

Psi(total) = Psi(s) + Psi(p) + Psi(g) + Psi(m)

ComponentMeaningMain effect
Psi(s), solute or osmotic potentialEffect of dissolved solutesAdding solutes lowers this component
Psi(p), pressure potentialHydrostatic pressure relative to the referencePositive in many turgid cells; can be negative in tension-bearing xylem
Psi(g), gravitational potentialEffect of heightRelevant across substantial vertical distances
Psi(m), matric potentialInteraction with surfaces or matricesImportant in soil and other adsorptive materials; commonly negative

Use component definitions consistently so that the same physical effect is not counted twice. For many small-scale cell questions, gravitational and matric terms can be neglected when the question states or implies this simplification.

Passive water movement is from higher total water potential to lower total water potential. A value of -0.2 MPa is higher than -0.8 MPa. Water movement responds to the total gradient, not to solute concentration alone. [12]

4.3 Osmosis, solutes and pressure

Osmosis is water movement across a selectively permeable membrane driven by a difference in water potential. Active solute transport can create a gradient; osmosis itself does not require ATP to pump water.

For an ideal dilute solution:

Psi(s) = -i x C x R x T

i is the effective number of dissolved particles per formula unit, C molar concentration, R the gas constant and T temperature in kelvin. With C in mol L^-1 and water potential in MPa, use R = 0.008314 L MPa mol^-1 K^-1. Electrolyte nonideality can make the simple ideal calculation inaccurate.

For 0.10 M sucrose at 298 K, taking i = 1 gives Psi(s) = -1 x 0.10 x 0.008314 x 298 = approximately -0.248 MPa.

The corresponding ideal osmotic pressure is Pi = i x C x R x T, a positive quantity in this convention, so Psi(s) = -Pi. Adding solute increases osmotic pressure while making osmotic potential more negative.

A cell with Psi(s) = -0.8 MPa and Psi(p) = +0.3 MPa has total Psi = -0.5 MPa if the other components are neglected. It initially gains water from an external solution at -0.2 MPa. As water enters, pressure and concentrations change until the total potentials equilibrate.

Equal water potentials do not require equal solute concentrations: a pressure difference can balance an osmotic difference.

4.4 Turgor, wilting and plasmolysis

Positive turgor pressure supports many plant tissues and contributes to cell expansion. Water loss can reduce turgor and cause wilting.

In a sufficiently hypertonic external medium, a plant cell can lose enough water for the protoplast to pull away from the cell wall: plasmolysis. At incipient plasmolysis, turgor pressure is approximately zero, making total water potential approximately equal to solute potential under the usual simplified assumptions.

Wilting of a whole plant and plasmolysis of individual cells are related to water status but are not identical definitions or obligatory simultaneous events.

4.5 The soil-plant-atmosphere continuum

During transpiration, water typically moves through a connected pathway from soil to roots, xylem, leaves and atmosphere. A decreasing water-potential gradient supports the flow. Cohesion and tension allow xylem water transport, while stomata regulate a major pathway of water-vapour loss.

Closing stomata can conserve water but restrict CO2 entry and reduce evaporative cooling. Carbon gain, leaf temperature and hydration are therefore linked. Severe drought can also impair water transport through embolism and other hydraulic limitations.

4.6 Relative humidity, vapour-pressure deficit and wind

Relative humidity (RH) compares actual water-vapour pressure with saturation vapour pressure at the same temperature:

RH (%) = 100 x actual vapour pressure / saturation vapour pressure

Vapour-pressure deficit (VPD) = saturation vapour pressure - actual vapour pressure.

For estimating the gradient from a leaf, use saturation vapour pressure at the leaf's temperature and actual vapour pressure of the surrounding air. A hotter leaf can experience a greater evaporative gradient than air-temperature calculations suggest.

At equal RH, warmer air generally has a larger saturation vapour pressure and hence a larger VPD. Thus identical RH does not mean identical evaporative demand. Wind can remove the moist boundary layer around a leaf and increase exchange, although stomatal responses and energy supply can modify the outcome. [13]

If saturation vapour pressure is 4.0 kPa and actual vapour pressure is 2.4 kPa, RH = 60% and VPD = 1.6 kPa.

4.7 Soil-water categories

  • Gravitational water drains from large pores under gravity after saturation.
  • Capillary water is retained in pores and films by capillary and surface forces; an important fraction can be available to plants.
  • Hygroscopic or very tightly adsorbed water is generally held too strongly for ordinary plant uptake.

Field capacity is the water content remaining after substantial free drainage from a previously wetted soil. Permanent wilting point is conventionally the water content at which a test plant fails to regain turgor under conditions limiting transpiration without addition of soil water. It is not a statement that rewatering can never restore the plant.

Values near -0.01 to -0.033 MPa are often used to approximate field capacity, and about -1.5 MPa to approximate permanent wilting point. These are operational approximations; texture, methods and plant characteristics matter.

Plant-available water capacity = water content at field capacity - water content at permanent wilting point. This concerns the extractable range, not all water stored in the soil. [14]

For volumetric water contents of 0.30 and 0.15 m^3 m^-3, available capacity is 0.15 m^3 m^-3. Over a uniform 1-m root zone, that represents 150 mm of water, before adjustments for stones, restricted rooting or uneven soil layers.

4.8 Water strategies and biological trade-offs

Plants can reduce drought exposure by completing sensitive stages during favourable periods, maintain access through rooting and storage, or tolerate lower tissue water status through physiological mechanisms. Xerophytes, mesophytes and hydrophytes describe broad associations with dry, intermediate and aquatic conditions; they are not precise measurements of one tolerance threshold.

Animals gain water through drinking, food and metabolic production, and lose it through excretion, evaporation and other routes. Terrestrial water balance depends on body coverings, behaviour, respiratory losses and excretory physiology. A strategy that saves water can carry energy, feeding or heat-dissipation costs.

Connection: to understand why the same amount of rainfall supports different plants in different places, we must examine the soil that stores water, supplies nutrients and surrounds the roots.

05 / Environment

Soil: a structured chemical and physical habitat

5.1 Soil develops through interacting processes

Soil formation, or pedogenesis, reflects parent material, climate, organisms, relief or topography, and time. Weathering supplies mineral material; organisms add and transform organic matter; water moves dissolved substances and particles; erosion and deposition alter the developing profile. These processes interact rather than acting independently. [32]

The rhizosphere is the soil region influenced by roots and their activities. Root uptake, exudates and associated microorganisms can make its chemical conditions different from those of the surrounding bulk soil.

Edaphic factors are soil-related environmental factors. They include texture, structure, depth, moisture, aeration, pH, salinity and nutrient availability.

5.2 Soil horizons and the profile

A soil profile is a vertical view of the soil and its layers. Master horizons and layers are commonly described as follows:

LabelMain character
OOrganic material, such as litter and partly decomposed residues
AMineral surface horizon commonly mixed with organic matter
EHorizon showing substantial eluviation, or removal of materials such as clay, iron or aluminium compounds
BSubsurface horizon with soil-forming alterations or accumulation of materials; often associated with illuviation
CRelatively little-altered parent material
RHard bedrock layer

Eluviation means movement out of a horizon; illuviation means accumulation in a horizon. An E horizon is not present in every soil, and a B horizon need not arise only by material accumulation. The complete O-A-E-B-C-R sequence is an explanatory model, not an obligatory profile. [16]

5.3 Texture: the particle-size mixture

Soil texture is the relative proportion of sand, silt and clay in the fine-earth fraction. Under the commonly used USDA convention:

  • Sand particles are approximately 0.05 to 2 mm in diameter.
  • Silt particles are approximately 0.002 to 0.05 mm.
  • Clay particles are less than 0.002 mm.

Size boundaries differ among classification systems, so identify the convention if a question supplies one. A soil-texture triangle uses percentages of sand, silt and clay that sum to 100% of the relevant fraction. Loam is a textural class, not a mixture of equal one-third proportions. [15]

Sandy soils commonly drain rapidly and have relatively low water and nutrient retention. Fine-textured soils commonly retain more total water and have greater surface area. However, a large fraction of clay-held water can be retained too tightly for plant uptake.

5.4 Structure: the arrangement of particles

Soil structure describes the arrangement of particles into aggregates and the pores between and within them. Examples include granular, blocky, platy and prismatic structures. Texture and structure are different: two soils can have similar particle-size proportions but different aggregation and pore continuity.

Large, connected pores facilitate drainage, gas exchange and root penetration. Smaller pores retain water. A soil's biological functioning depends on the balance and connectivity of these pores, not simply the total quantity of empty space.

Compaction can raise resistance to root growth, reduce large pores and restrict infiltration and aeration. Water-holding capacity alone therefore does not describe a good root environment.

5.5 Bulk density, particle density and porosity

Bulk density is the dry mass of soil divided by total bulk volume, including pores. Particle density is the mass of solid particles divided by their solid volume, excluding pores.

For a compatible set of measurements:

Porosity fraction = 1 - bulk density / particle density

For bulk density 1.30 g cm^-3 and particle density 2.65 g cm^-3, porosity = 1 - 1.30/2.65 = 0.509, or approximately 50.9%.

The commonly used mineral-particle density of 2.65 g cm^-3 is an approximation. Organic-rich soils and soils with different mineral compositions can differ. High porosity does not, on its own, tell us whether the pores provide useful aeration or plant-available water. [17]

5.6 Water retention and aeration must be considered together

As soil dries, water remains in progressively smaller pores and thinner films, and its matric potential becomes more negative. A water-retention curve plots water content against suction or water potential. The sign convention on the horizontal axis must be checked.

A clay-rich soil can hold more water than a sandy soil at the same potential and still have less readily extractable water than a well-structured loam over the operational available-water range. This is why total moisture content, field capacity and available water must be distinguished. [14]

When water fills most pores, atmospheric oxygen diffuses into the soil much more slowly. Roots and microorganisms can consume the remaining oxygen, creating hypoxia or anoxia. Thus dry soil can impose water shortage, while saturated soil can impose oxygen shortage.

5.7 Soil colloids and cation exchange capacity

Clay minerals and organic colloids provide charged surfaces that retain and exchange ions. Many important soil surfaces have a net negative charge and can retain nutrient cations such as Ca2+, Mg2+, K+ and NH4+.

Cation exchange capacity (CEC) measures the capacity to hold exchangeable positive charge. It is commonly reported as cmol of charge per kg, written cmol(+) kg^-1, or as milliequivalents per 100 g; these two units have the same numerical value.

CEC depends on the amount and type of clay, organic matter and, for variable-charge surfaces, pH. It is not simply proportional to clay percentage. Exchangeable ions can return to soil solution; retention is not necessarily permanent fixation. [18]

Nitrate, NO3-, is not retained by ordinary cation-exchange sites and can be relatively mobile. Phosphate is also an anion, but it can be strongly retained by other surface reactions or precipitated: not all anions behave like nitrate.

A high CEC does not guarantee high fertility. The identity of the ions occupying the exchange sites matters. Strong acidity or excessive exchangeable sodium can accompany an unfavourable root environment despite substantial exchange capacity.

5.8 Organic matter, nutrients and biological processes

Organic matter contributes to nutrient storage, aggregate stability and water retention. Decomposition releases some nutrients through mineralisation, while microbes can temporarily take up mineral nutrients into their biomass through immobilisation. Fresh material with a high carbon:nitrogen ratio can promote microbial competition for available nitrogen, depending on its decomposability and other conditions.

Water and temperature affect these processes, while oxygen changes which pathways are possible. Nitrification generally requires aerobic conditions; denitrification is favoured in oxygen-limited microsites with nitrate and suitable electron donors. Root uptake, microbial transformation and leaching jointly influence nutrient supply. [33]

The total amount of an element is not the same as the amount available to a plant. Chemical form, adsorption, precipitation, pH, redox state and biological access all matter.

5.9 Soil pH and redox conditions

Strongly acidic conditions can increase aluminium solubility and cause toxicity in susceptible plants. At high pH, availability of some micronutrients such as iron can decline. Phosphorus can be poorly available in both strongly acidic soils and calcium-rich alkaline soils through different retention processes.

Redox potential reflects the tendency of a system to accept or donate electrons. When oxygen is depleted, microbes can use alternative electron acceptors. Reduction of nitrate, manganese and iron compounds, and under sufficiently reducing conditions sulfate, changes nutrient forms, metal mobility and possible toxic products.

This is a conceptual sequence of increasing reduction, not a rigid spatial order that must appear everywhere: microsites, substrates, pH and transport can alter the pattern.

Connection: a soil can be moist and nutrient-rich yet remain stressful because of dissolved salts. Salinity changes both water relations and ion balance.

06 / Environment

Salinity: the water-and-ion problem

6.1 What salinity measures

Salinity describes dissolved salts in water or another medium. It can be expressed as a mass fraction, such as g kg^-1. Ocean water commonly has a salinity near 35 g of salts per kg of seawater, but salinity varies among locations and with freshwater inputs and evaporation. [23]

Practical Salinity is a conductivity-based, formally dimensionless quantity; it should not be treated as an exact synonym for a mass concentration in g L^-1. In routine ecological questions, read the stated units and convention.

Evaporation and freezing can concentrate salts in the remaining liquid. Rainfall, river input and melting dilute salts, while tidal mixing produces fluctuating estuarine conditions. Soil salinity can increase where salts accumulate through irrigation, evaporation or inadequate drainage.

Water activity describes the effective availability of water for processes such as microbial growth. It is related to the vapour pressure of water over a sample relative to that over pure water at the same temperature. High salt can lower water activity even when much liquid water is present.

6.2 Osmotic effects and ion-specific effects

Adding solute lowers the water potential of an external solution. An organism may therefore lose water to a saline medium or find it difficult to obtain water from it.

Plant salt stress has two major components:

  • Osmotic stress: a lowered external water potential restricts water uptake and can rapidly reduce growth.
  • Ion-specific stress: accumulated ions can damage metabolism, disrupt nutrient balance and cause toxicity. For example, excessive sodium can interfere with potassium-dependent functions.

The osmotic and ionic components are related but distinguishable. A soil can be physically wet while imposing water stress through its low osmotic potential. [19]

6.3 Osmolarity and tonicity are not identical

Osmolarity counts osmotically active particles per litre of solution. Osmolality counts them per kilogram of solvent. Osmoles measure particles, not merely the mass of salt.

Tonicity concerns the sustained effect of a solution on cell volume and depends on solutes that do not readily cross the membrane under the conditions considered. Two iso-osmotic solutions need not be isotonic if their solutes differ in permeability.

The ideal van 't Hoff calculation is useful for dilute solutions, but seawater and strongly saline media may require nonideal treatment. Use the assumptions supplied in a calculation.

6.4 Osmoregulation versus osmoconformity

Osmoregulators maintain internal osmotic conditions relatively independently of the external medium over a range. Osmoconformers allow total internal osmotic concentration to vary more closely with the environment.

An osmoconformer can still regulate particular ions and maintain intracellular conditions. Similar total osmotic concentration does not require identical ionic composition or an absence of active transport. Regulation is also a matter of degree and depends on which compartment and range are measured. [21]

6.5 Freshwater and marine bony fishes face opposite net challenges

The following comparison describes typical teleost strategies, not every aquatic animal:

FeatureTypical freshwater teleostTypical marine teleost
Body fluids relative to mediumMore concentratedLess concentrated
Net passive water tendencyWater entersWater leaves
Net ion challengeLoss of salts to dilute surroundingsGain of salts, including those in ingested seawater
DrinkingUsually littleUsually substantial seawater drinking
UrineRelatively large volume, diluteRelatively small volume; particular ions also excreted by the kidney
Gill functionActive uptake of important ionsActive elimination of excess monovalent ions

Fish can alter transport processes in gills, intestine and kidney when adjusting to different salinities. These changes involve coordinated physiology and can take time. A rapid transfer can be harmful even in a species capable of long-term acclimation to both environments. [21,22]

Marine sharks illustrate a different solution. Retaining urea and other osmolytes helps bring body-fluid osmotic concentration close to, or slightly above, that of seawater. Trimethylamine N-oxide, TMAO, can counter some disruptive effects of urea on proteins. Sharks still regulate salts; the rectal gland contributes to salt secretion.

6.6 Euryhaline and stenohaline organisms

Euryhaline organisms tolerate a relatively broad salinity range. Stenohaline organisms tolerate a relatively narrow range. A freshwater specialist and a marine specialist can both be stenohaline: the term describes breadth, not whether the optimum is high or low.

Many salmon and eels illustrate life cycles requiring substantial shifts in salinity exposure. Euryhalinity can support use of estuaries or movement between freshwater and seawater, but life stage and acclimation history influence the actual tolerable range.

6.7 Salt tolerance in plants

Halophytes are adapted to saline environments; glycophytes generally lack that degree of salt tolerance. Salt tolerance depends on tissue, life stage, concentration, duration and the salts involved.

Mechanisms can include root-level exclusion, regulated transport, secretion by specialised structures, storage in older tissues, succulence, and compartmentation of ions in vacuoles. Accumulation of compatible organic solutes, such as proline or glycine betaine, can help maintain osmotic balance while protecting cytoplasmic functions.

Osmotic adjustment is not simply allowing toxic salt to accumulate freely in the cytoplasm. Ion location and protection of sensitive processes are essential. The detailed adaptation examples will be developed in A5.

Mangrove habitats also combine salinity with waterlogging and low sediment oxygen. Salt tolerance alone therefore does not explain all features of mangrove roots or distribution.

6.8 Salinity and sodicity are different soil problems

Salinity concerns total soluble salts. Sodicity concerns the dominance of exchangeable sodium relative to other exchangeable cations.

Electrical conductivity, EC, is commonly used as a salinity indicator because dissolved ions conduct electricity. It depends on temperature and ion composition; a single universal EC-to-salt-concentration conversion is inappropriate. Soil saturated-paste extract conductivity is written ECe, and dS m^-1 is a common unit.

Exchangeable sodium percentage, ESP = 100 x exchangeable sodium / CEC, using compatible charge-based units. High exchangeable sodium can disperse clay, damage soil structure and restrict infiltration. A saline soil need not be sodic, and sodium-related structural problems cannot be diagnosed from pH alone. [20]

The sodium adsorption ratio (SAR) compares sodium with calcium and magnesium in water or an extract: SAR = [Na+] / sqrt{([Ca2+] + [Mg2+])/2}, using the specified equivalent-based concentrations, conventionally meq L^-1. SAR describes the solution's relative sodium hazard; ESP describes the exchange complex. Do not substitute raw mg L^-1 values into this formula without the required ion conversions.

Connection: salt concentration describes how much dissolved ionic material is present, but pH helps determine the chemical form, availability and toxicity of particular substances.

07 / Environment

pH: chemical form, buffering and biological effect

7.1 What pH means

Formally, pH = -log10(aH+), where aH+ is hydrogen-ion activity. In sufficiently dilute solutions, hydrogen-ion concentration is often used as an approximation.

Lower pH means higher hydrogen-ion activity. A difference of one pH unit represents a tenfold difference; a difference of two units represents a hundredfold difference. For solutions A and B, the ratio aH+(A)/aH+(B) = 10^[pH(B) - pH(A)]. [24]

Water at pH 6.5 has 100 times the hydrogen-ion activity of water at pH 8.5. The difference is not merely two units on a linear concentration scale.

7.2 Neutrality depends on temperature

Neutrality means equal hydrogen-ion and hydroxide-ion activities. At about 25 C in dilute water, neutral pH is approximately 7. The ion product of water varies with temperature, so neutral pH is not always exactly 7.

The familiar 0-to-14 scale is useful for common dilute aqueous systems, but it is not an absolute mathematical limit on all possible pH values. Measurement conditions and activity effects matter in concentrated solutions.

7.3 Direct and indirect biological effects

pH influences protein charge, enzyme activity, membrane transport and the maintenance of intracellular conditions. External pH can therefore affect growth, survival and community composition, even when organisms regulate internal pH.

pH also changes chemical speciation: the relative amounts of different chemical forms of an element or compound. This can alter nutrient availability, metal solubility and toxicity. More total material does not necessarily mean more of its biologically active form.

Organisms differ in pH preferences. Acidophilic, neutrophilic and alkaliphilic organisms favour relatively acidic, near-neutral and alkaline conditions respectively. Exact boundaries and tolerance ranges vary. A preference and a survival limit are not the same measurement.

7.4 Buffering and alkalinity

A buffer resists pH change when acid or base is added. Buffering depends on the amounts and chemical properties of acid-base components, not merely the starting pH.

Alkalinity describes acid-neutralising capacity and is commonly measured by acid titration. In many natural waters, bicarbonate and carbonate make important contributions. High alkalinity does not mean the same thing as a high pH. [25]

Two ponds can both have pH 7.5 but respond differently to the same acid input because their alkalinities differ. Hardness, which largely reflects calcium and magnesium, is another distinct property; it is not a synonym for pH, alkalinity or salinity.

7.5 CO2 links pH to biological activity

A simplified carbonate-system sequence is:

CO2 + H2O <=> H2CO3 <=> H+ + HCO3-

Additional equilibria connect bicarbonate, carbonate and hydrogen ions. Respiration adds CO2 and can lower pH. Photosynthetic CO2 removal can raise pH. In a productive pond, pH may rise during the day and fall at night, with the magnitude strongly affected by buffering and mixing.

When seawater takes up CO2, hydrogen-ion activity rises and carbonate-ion availability can decline. This can make calcium-carbonate formation or maintenance more difficult for susceptible calcifying organisms. Ocean acidification means a reduction in pH; seawater does not have to fall below pH 7 for acidification to occur. [26]

7.6 pH, soil and freshwater acidification

Acid inputs can displace nutrient cations and increase the mobility of some metals. Aluminium toxicity is particularly important in many acidic soil and freshwater contexts. Other metals respond differently depending on their chemistry, organic binding and redox conditions; avoid a rule that every metal behaves identically.

Naturally acidic waters also occur. Rain equilibrated with CO2 is weakly acidic, and organic acids can lower pH in particular habitats. A pH below 7, by itself, does not establish pollution.

For plants, calcicoles are associated with calcium-rich, often calcareous substrates, whereas calcifuges avoid such substrates. Such distributions reflect interacting nutrient and soil-chemistry conditions, not a direct reading of one pH value alone.

7.7 Ammonia: a useful speciation example

Total ammonia includes un-ionised NH3 and ionised NH4+. The un-ionised form readily crosses biological membranes and is especially important in aquatic toxicity.

At fixed temperature and total ammonia, increasing pH increases the NH3 fraction. Temperature also changes the equilibrium, so an ammonia interpretation should consider both pH and temperature rather than total ammonia alone. Nitrification can additionally consume oxygen and generate acidity. [27]

Under comparable ordinary freshwater conditions, warming generally increases the un-ionised fraction. A warm, high-pH situation can therefore be more hazardous than a cool, lower-pH situation with the same total ammonia.

For a supplied pKa and compatible conditions:

Fraction present as NH3 = 1 / [1 + 10^(pKa - pH)]

If pKa is given as 9.25, the NH3 fraction at pH 7.25 is 1/101, approximately 0.99%. At pH 9.25 it is 50%. These are fractions of total ammonia; calculate a concentration only with a clearly stated and consistent mass basis.

Connection: pH changes what chemical forms organisms encounter. Oxygen determines which energy-yielding processes can operate and is itself altered by photosynthesis, respiration, mixing and temperature.

08 / Environment

Oxygen: supply, demand and transport

8.1 Oxygen availability is more than oxygen percentage

Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor in aerobic respiration. It must reach the relevant tissues or microorganisms fast enough to meet demand.

Dry air contains approximately 21% oxygen by volume. At altitude, this fraction remains approximately similar while total atmospheric pressure decreases. Oxygen partial pressure consequently decreases:

pO2 = oxygen mole fraction x total gas pressure

At 100 kPa total pressure, a 0.21 oxygen fraction gives pO2 = 21 kPa. At 60 kPa, it gives 12.6 kPa. Humidified gas requires the appropriate water-vapour correction when a question includes it.

In water, dissolved oxygen (DO) refers to molecular O2 dissolved in the medium, commonly reported in mg L^-1 or as percent saturation. The oxygen atoms chemically bound in H2O are not the dissolved O2 extracted by fish gills. [28,34]

8.2 What controls oxygen solubility?

At equilibrium, gas solubility depends on temperature, salinity and gas partial pressure. For a fixed temperature and medium, a Henry-law form is C = kH x pO2, where this particular kH convention expresses concentration per unit pressure. Other Henry-constant conventions use the reciprocal, so inspect units.

Under otherwise similar conditions:

  • Warmer water holds less dissolved oxygen at saturation.
  • More saline water holds less dissolved oxygen at saturation.
  • Higher oxygen partial pressure increases equilibrium dissolved oxygen.

Actual DO also depends on production, consumption, mixing and exchange. A photosynthesising water body can temporarily exceed 100% saturation. Conversely, water can remain far below its equilibrium capacity because biological demand exceeds replenishment. [28,30]

Percent oxygen saturation = 100 x observed DO / equilibrium DO at the local temperature, salinity and pressure.

If measured DO is 7.2 mg L^-1 and the appropriate saturation value is 9.0 mg L^-1, saturation is 80%. Do not use a sea-level freshwater saturation value indiscriminately for a warm saline or high-altitude sample.

8.3 Diffusion creates transport constraints

For a simple steady concentration gradient, the magnitude of diffusive transfer follows:

Transfer rate = D x area x concentration difference / diffusion distance

D is a diffusion coefficient. Greater exchange area and a steeper gradient increase transfer; a thicker barrier reduces it. The negative sign in the full Fick-law expression indicates movement down a concentration gradient.

Oxygen's diffusion coefficient in water is roughly four orders of magnitude smaller than in air near ordinary environmental temperatures. Exact ratios depend on conditions. This is one reason why water-filled soil pores and thick stagnant boundary layers can strongly restrict oxygen delivery. [29]

Convection and mixing can transport oxygen over larger distances and maintain gradients for local diffusion. Moving water may increase exchange at a surface, but observed oxygen still depends on sources and sinks.

8.4 Oxygen sources and sinks in water

Sources include exchange with the atmosphere, oxygenic photosynthesis and oxygen-bearing inflows. Sinks include respiration by animals, plants and microorganisms, decomposition and oxidation of reduced substances.

Algae and aquatic plants respire in both light and darkness; photosynthesis supplies oxygen only when suitable light and physiological conditions permit it. In a productive, reasonably mixed pond, DO often rises during daylight and falls overnight, with a minimum near dawn. Weather, inflows, mixing and stratification can change this pattern.

An algal bloom can therefore coexist with low nighttime or deep-water oxygen. Large biomass is not proof that oxygen is continuously abundant.

8.5 Warming, stratification and eutrophication interact

Warming can reduce oxygen solubility while increasing biological oxygen demand within a suitable temperature range. Stable stratification restricts resupply of deeper water, where respiration and decomposition can continue.

Nutrient enrichment can increase algal production. Settling organic material and subsequent decomposition can consume oxygen below the surface. Oxygen depletion thus reflects the balance of supply, demand and transport, not nutrients acting as a direct oxygen-removing chemical in every case.

Hypoxia means low oxygen relative to a defined criterion; anoxia means effectively absent oxygen. A DO value below about 2 mg L^-1 is a widely used coastal hypoxia convention, not a universal threshold of survival for every organism or life stage. [28]

8.6 Organisms differ in their response to oxygen

  • Obligate aerobes require oxygen for their normal energy metabolism.
  • Facultative anaerobes can shift between oxygen-dependent and oxygen-independent pathways, depending on their capabilities.
  • Obligate anaerobes cannot tolerate ordinary oxygen exposure to varying degrees.
  • Microaerophiles require or favour oxygen below atmospheric levels.

Anaerobic respiration uses a terminal electron acceptor other than oxygen, such as nitrate in suitable organisms. Fermentation relies on internal organic electron acceptors and does not use a respiratory electron-transport chain in the same sense. They are not interchangeable terms for every process occurring without oxygen.

Animals can respond to low oxygen by changing ventilation, circulation, activity or habitat use. Respiratory pigments can increase oxygen transport, but the response depends on pigment properties and physiology. A high oxygen affinity can help loading at low pO2 while making unloading more difficult under other conditions.

8.7 Flooded roots and wetland plants

Flooding reduces gas exchange through the soil. Roots may become ATP-limited as aerobic respiration is restricted, impairing nutrient uptake and water transport even though water surrounds them.

Some wetland plants possess aerenchyma, tissues with extensive internal gas spaces that facilitate gas movement. Some mangrove species possess specialised aerial roots, including pneumatophores, that improve access to atmospheric gases. These are responses to oxygen conditions as well as habitat structure; not all aquatic or mangrove species use the same root form.

Reduced soils may accumulate substances such as sulfide that impose additional stress. Waterlogging is therefore not simply the opposite of drought: the two stresses restrict different parts of normal function.

8.8 DO, BOD and COD measure different things

DO is the oxygen present at the time of measurement. Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) estimates oxygen consumption during a specified biological incubation. Chemical oxygen demand (COD) estimates oxygen-equivalent consumption in a specified chemical oxidation procedure.

The conventional BOD5 test uses a five-day incubation at 20 C, in darkness to avoid photosynthetic oxygen production. BOD can include nitrogenous oxygen demand unless the method excludes or inhibits it; carbonaceous BOD is a more specific measurement. [30]

In a simplified dilution calculation, with appropriate blank and seed corrections assumed unnecessary or already made:

BOD5 = (initial bottle DO - final bottle DO) / sample volume fraction

If initial DO is 8.0 mg L^-1, final DO is 3.0 mg L^-1, and the bottle contains 20% sample by volume, BOD5 = (8 - 3)/0.20 = 25 mg L^-1 of original sample.

The assay must retain adequate oxygen and satisfy its method requirements. A bottle exhausted of oxygen before the endpoint cannot reveal the full demand without an appropriate dilution or other method adjustment. [30,31]

COD commonly exceeds BOD5 because a chemical test can oxidise material not biologically consumed within five days. However, test chemistry, inhibitors and substances present affect the relationship. High BOD is not high DO, and a low BOD does not prove that every toxic pollutant is absent.

8.9 Interpreting an oxygen sag

Downstream of a substantial biodegradable organic input, decomposition can initially consume oxygen faster than it is replenished. DO declines and then can recover as demand diminishes and reaeration or photosynthesis replenishes oxygen.

The location and severity of the minimum depend on flow, temperature, mixing, loading and biological processes. The largest oxygen deficit need not occur exactly at the discharge point. This is a rate-and-transport explanation, not simply a map of pollutant concentration.

Connection: oxygen reveals the central lesson of the whole subsection: environmental factors work through each other. The next step is to combine them rather than interpret each as an isolated label.

09 / Connect the concepts

Integrating factors: worked applications

The values in these examples are original teaching datasets. Interpret the stated assumptions rather than treating the numbers as universal biological thresholds or as verified previous-year questions.

9.1 Light attenuation and compensation depth

Suppose I0 = 1,000 micromoles m^-2 s^-1 and k = 0.40 m^-1. At depth 4 m:

I(4) = 1,000 x exp(-0.40 x 4) = approximately 202 micromoles m^-2 s^-1.

If the organism's compensation irradiance is 20 micromoles m^-2 s^-1 and other conditions remain appropriate:

Compensation depth = ln(I0 / Icompensation) / k = ln(50)/0.40 = approximately 9.78 m.

The 1%-of-surface-light depth is ln(100)/0.40, approximately 11.51 m. These depths differ because compensation in this example requires 2%, not 1%, of surface irradiance.

9.2 Light and dark bottles: oxygen-based production

Identical closed water samples begin at 6 mg O2 L^-1. After one hour, a light bottle contains 8 mg L^-1 and a dark bottle contains 4 mg L^-1.

  • Net oxygen production in light = (8 - 6)/1 = 2 mg O2 L^-1 h^-1.
  • Respiratory oxygen consumption estimated from darkness = (6 - 4)/1 = 2 mg O2 L^-1 h^-1.
  • Gross oxygen production estimated by adding these = 2 + 2 = 4 mg O2 L^-1 h^-1.

This calculation assumes comparable respiration in light and dark, no gas exchange with the atmosphere, and no important unaccounted oxygen sources or sinks. In a natural mixed community, the net measurement includes heterotrophic respiration and is strictly net community production, not automatically plant-only net primary production. Oxygen-to-carbon conversion requires an additional stoichiometric assumption.

9.3 A thermal refuge may fail because of oxygen

Suppose a fish requires temperatures from 20 to 24 C and DO of at least 4 mg L^-1 under the conditions specified.

Water layerTemperatureDO
Surface28 C8 mg L^-1
Middle22 C5 mg L^-1
Deep21 C1 mg L^-1

Only the middle layer meets both supplied criteria. The surface has oxygen but is too warm; the deep layer is thermally suitable but too oxygen-poor. Habitat suitability is the intersection of requirements, not the location where any one factor looks favourable.

In nature, food, predators, access and other conditions can further restrict use of that layer. The stated requirements are hypothetical values for this example.

9.4 A factorial experiment reveals an interaction

Consider mean plant growth in arbitrary units after a standard exposure:

TemperatureLow salinityHigh salinity
20 C106
30 C81

At 20 C, high salinity reduces growth by 4 units. At 30 C, it reduces growth by 7 units. The effect of salinity depends on temperature: the response is nonadditive on this measurement scale.

If the salinity effect stayed at -4 units, the predicted combined-treatment value would be 8 - 4 = 4. The observed value is 1, or 3 units lower. Replication, uncertainty estimates and a suitable statistical model are needed before claiming a reliable interaction from experimental data.

To isolate mechanisms, control relevant variables and standardise life stage and exposure history. Merely holding RH constant while changing temperature does not hold VPD constant. Record the physiological conditions, not just treatment labels.

9.5 Constant means can conceal stressful extremes

Two habitats can have the same average temperature while one experiences damaging afternoon peaks. Two soils can have the same water content but different water potentials. Two ponds can have the same pH but different alkalinity. Two waters can have the same DO concentration but different percent saturation.

The common reasoning step is to identify the quantity that actually determines the process. An average, total or starting value is often insufficient.

10.2 Recognising the main graph types

  • Thermal performance: identify the measured trait, optimum, curve breadth and the nature of the high-temperature decline. Do not equate a functional limit with a lethal limit.

  • Photosynthesis versus light: distinguish the negative dark exchange, compensation point, saturation region and possible photoinhibition.
  • Light versus depth: exponential attenuation becomes linear when ln(I/I0) is plotted against depth; slope is -k.
  • Soil water-retention curve: inspect whether the horizontal axis shows negative potential, positive suction or its logarithm before interpreting direction.
  • Osmotic concentration versus external salinity: a relatively flat internal response suggests regulation over that range; a response tracking the external medium suggests conformity in the measured quantity.
  • pH axis: equal numerical intervals represent multiplicative changes in hydrogen-ion activity.
  • Diel oxygen or pH: connect timing to photosynthesis, respiration and mixing; evaluate depth and buffering before assuming the same pattern everywhere.

The next connection: ecological factors and organism responses

We have now examined what temperature, light, water, soil, salinity, pH and oxygen do, how they are measured and why their effects are connected.

The next question is how to organise these influences from an organism's point of view. Some environmental features are used and can be depleted; others describe conditions under which biological processes must operate. Organisms can regulate internal states, conform to external changes, move, change activity or enter a different physiological state.

In A3: Ecological Factors and Organism Responses, we connect abiotic and biotic influences, distinguish conditions from resources and examine how an organism's response emerges from interacting factors. The limiting-factor laws and detailed tolerance framework follow in A4, with ecological adaptations developed further in A5.

The thread remains the same: a habitat supports an organism when its conditions, resources and relationships permit that organism to maintain function, survive and reproduce.
