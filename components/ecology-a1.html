A1 · ECOLOGY FOUNDATIONS

Ecology and Levels of Organisation

Begin with one organism. Understand the connections that make a living world. Scope: organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere. One pond, five lenses to understand life's organization.

POND STORY

Imagine standing beside a pond.

A fish moves through the water. Microscopic algae capture sunlight. Insects feed near the surface. Bacteria and fungi break down dead material. Oxygen, nutrients and water move through this living system.

Each organism has its own life, but its survival depends on conditions and relationships beyond itself.

The fish needs suitable water temperature and enough dissolved oxygen. Its food supply depends on other organisms. Its waste becomes part of the environment experienced by those organisms.

Ecology helps us understand life by studying its relationships - with other life and with the physical world.

What is ecology?

Ecology is the scientific study of interactions among organisms and between organisms and their environment.

It investigates how these interactions influence the distribution, abundance and functioning of living systems. This includes the movement and transformation of energy and matter. [1]

Let us unpack those ideas.

2.1 Distribution: where organisms occur

Distribution describes the places in which an organism or species is found.

For example, a particular aquatic plant may occur in shallow parts of a pond but be absent from deeper water.

The ecological question is:

What explains this pattern?

Possible explanations include differences in light availability, water movement, sediment conditions or interactions with other organisms.

2.2 Abundance: how many occur

Abundance describes the number of individuals present in a specified place.

A species may occur in two ponds but be abundant in one and rare in the other.

Its presence tells us that it occurs there. Its abundance tells us how many individuals occur there.

Distribution and abundance are related, but they describe different things.

2.3 Interactions: how organisms affect one another

Organisms may:

  • Consume other organisms.
  • Compete for resources.
  • Provide food, shelter or protection.
  • Spread disease.
  • Modify conditions experienced by other organisms.

These interactions help explain why a species succeeds, declines or persists in a particular place.

2.4 Processes: how living systems work

Ecology also examines processes such as reproduction, dispersal, production of organic matter, decomposition and nutrient cycling.

A useful distinction is:

TermMeaningPond example
PatternAn observable arrangement or differenceMore aquatic plants occur near the shore
ProcessAn activity or mechanism that can produce a patternGreater light availability supports plant growth in shallow water

BioSins connection

A strong ecological explanation connects an observed pattern to the processes that could produce it.

A brief origin

The word ecology comes from the Greek oikos, meaning home or household, and logos, meaning study. Ernst Haeckel introduced the term in 1866. [2]

The central idea remains useful: organisms live within a shared household of conditions, resources and relationships.

What does "environment" include?

An organism's environment includes the external conditions and influences that affect its life.

For ecological study, we commonly distinguish two broad components.

Abiotic components

These are the nonliving physical and chemical features of the environment.

Examples include temperature, light, water availability, salinity, pH and dissolved oxygen.

Biotic components

These involve living organisms and their activities.

Examples include competitors, predators, prey, parasites, hosts and mutualistic partners.

A fish therefore experiences both the physical properties of water and the activities of other organisms.

These components interact. Organisms respond to physical conditions, and their activities can modify those conditions. For example, photosynthesis and respiration influence oxygen availability.

Ecology includes these relationships in forests, oceans, farms, cities and microscopic environments. Humans are also part of ecological systems. [3]

Why study life at different levels?

The pond contains many possible subjects of study.

We could examine the response of one fish to temperature. We could count all individuals of its species. We could study interactions among different species. Or we could investigate the movement of nutrients through the whole pond system.

Each approach reveals something different.

A level of ecological organisation identifies the biological system on which a question focuses.

LevelMain focusExample
OrganismAn individual and its environmental relationshipsOne fish responding to low oxygen
PopulationIndividuals of the same species in a defined areaA fish species' population in the pond
CommunityPopulations of different species and their interactionsThe pond's plants, animals and microorganisms

Organism, population, community and ecosystem are widely used focal levels of ecological research. Landscape, biome and biosphere extend our view to broader spatial patterns and connections. [4]

We will now build these levels one at a time.

Organism: the individual in its environment

An organism is an individual living entity.

At this level, ecology asks how an individual obtains resources, tolerates conditions, avoids danger and reproduces.

Return to our fish.

Its ability to survive in the pond depends partly on how its body functions and how it behaves. It may move between depths, change its activity or occupy a different part of the pond as conditions change.

Questions at the organism level

  • How does temperature influence an individual's activity?
  • How does a plant maintain water balance?
  • How does an animal select a feeding location?
  • Which traits help an organism survive in a particular environment?

Organismal ecology draws on physiology, morphology and behaviour.

  • Physiology concerns how the body functions.
  • Morphology concerns form and structure.
  • Behaviour concerns the actions of organisms.

For instance, a leaf's structure can influence water loss, while an animal's behaviour can influence its exposure to heat.

Where ecology meets physiology

Measuring an enzyme's temperature response is a physiological investigation.

Using that response to understand why an organism can survive in one environment but fails in another connects physiology to ecology.

The ecological focus is the consequence for life in an environmental setting.

NET focus

An organism may change its behaviour or physiology during its lifetime. Such a response does not automatically represent evolutionary change. We will distinguish individual responses from evolutionary adaptations in A5.

Population: one species, many individuals

A population consists of individuals of the same species occupying a defined area at a particular time, with the potential to interact.

A useful population description therefore identifies:

  1. Which species?
  2. Which area?
  3. Which time or period?

"All individuals of fish species X in Pond P during the breeding season" is more precise than simply saying "the fish population."

6.1 Why the same-species condition matters

Suppose a pond contains three fish species.

All the fish together do not form one population. Each species has its own population within the pond.

Similarly:

  • All individuals of one grass species in a field can form a population.
  • All plants of different species in that field do not form one population.

A species can also contain many populations across its geographical range.

6.2 What population ecology studies

Population ecology examines the size, structure and changes of populations.

Important attributes include:

AttributeWhat it describes
Population sizeTotal number of individuals
Population densityNumber of individuals per unit area or volume
Age structureRepresentation of different age classes
Sex ratioRelative representation of sexes, where applicable
DispersionSpatial arrangement of individuals
Demographic ratesBirths, deaths and other processes influencing population change

Population size and density are distinct: the same number of individuals can occupy different areas and therefore have different densities. [5]

Population size changes through births, deaths, immigration and emigration. We will examine these processes in detail in Population Ecology.

6.3 A population has properties that an individual does not

An individual fish has an age.

A population has an age structure.

An individual may reproduce.

A population has a pattern of reproduction and recruitment through time.

The change in focus - from one individual to a group - allows us to ask questions that cannot be answered by studying one organism alone.

A useful qualification

Definitions sometimes emphasise interbreeding. Reproductive connections are important in sexually reproducing populations, but population ecology also studies asexual organisms.

Do not make "every individual must interbreed" a universal requirement.

Community: different populations connected

A biological community consists of populations of different species occurring in an area and connected through actual or potential interactions.

The pond community includes its plants, algae, animals, fungi, bacteria and other organisms.

The emphasis shifts from:

"How does one species' population change?"

to:

"How do different species influence one another and the organisation of the community?"

7.1 Communities contain networks of relationships

Some interactions are direct.

A fish eating an insect directly affects that insect.

Other effects are indirect.

If a predator reduces the abundance of a herbivore, the plants eaten by that herbivore may experience less grazing.

Thus, two species can influence one another without direct contact. Community ecology investigates these networks and their consequences. [6]

7.2 What community ecology studies

Important questions concern:

  • Which species occur together?
  • How many species are present?
  • How are individuals distributed among species?
  • Which species dominate?
  • How do interactions influence coexistence?
  • How does community composition change through time?

Two basic ideas will recur throughout the unit:

Species richness is the number of species present.

Species composition describes which species are present.

Two communities can have the same richness but contain completely different species.

Community boundaries depend on the question

We may study a whole pond community or a defined portion of it, such as the microbial community in its sediment.

A study of a plant community intentionally focuses on plants. The complete biological community also includes other groups of organisms.

NET focus

A community is the living component of a system. However, community ecology can still investigate how temperature, nutrients or other abiotic factors influence that community.

Ecosystem: life and the physical environment functioning together

An ecosystem is a biological community interacting with its physical and chemical environment.

The pond ecosystem includes its organisms, water, dissolved substances, sediment and the exchanges connecting them.

The interactions are essential. An ecosystem is more than a list of living and nonliving objects.

For example:

  • Algae absorb nutrients from the water.
  • Consumers obtain organic matter by feeding.
  • Organisms release waste.
  • Decomposition returns elements to environmental pools.
  • Photosynthesis and respiration affect gases dissolved in the water.

These processes connect organisms to the nonliving environment.

8.1 Structure and function

Ecosystem structure concerns what is present and how it is organised.

Examples include organisms, feeding relationships and pools of organic matter or nutrients.

Ecosystem function concerns what happens.

Examples include production, consumption, decomposition and nutrient transfers.

8.2 Ecosystems have no fixed size

A small water-filled depression can be studied as an ecosystem. So can a lake, forest or much larger region.

The useful boundary depends on the process and question being investigated. Ecosystems can therefore be small or large. [7]

8.3 Energy and matter behave differently

Two principles will organise much of Ecosystem Ecology.

Energy flows through ecological systems.

Energy enters, is transferred and transformed, and becomes increasingly dispersed as heat. Energy is conserved, but it is not continually recycled through a food web in the way nutrient elements can be.

Matter moves among biological and nonbiological pools.

Elements such as carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus can be taken up, incorporated into organisms, released and reused.

This distinction also operates at the Earth-system scale: energy passes through the system while matter moves among interconnected reservoirs. [8]

8.4 Most ecosystems exchange both energy and matter

A pond receives sunlight, rainwater, nutrients and organisms. It loses heat, water, materials and organisms.

It is therefore an open system.

Nutrient cycling does not mean that every nutrient remains permanently inside one ecosystem.

An exception worth remembering

Sunlight supports most primary production, but it is not the immediate energy source for every food web.

At hydrothermal vents and some other environments, chemosynthetic microorganisms use energy from chemical reactions to produce organic matter. [9]

Landscape, biome and biosphere: widening the view

9.1 Landscape: connections among patches

A landscape contains a spatial mosaic of ecological patches.

Imagine several ponds surrounded by agricultural fields, grassland and forest.

The arrangement matters.

Two ponds may contain suitable conditions for the same species, yet movement between them may depend on the intervening land.

Landscape ecology examines how spatial patterns influence ecological processes, including movement and exchanges among patches. [10]

Its key addition is spatial arrangement and connectivity.

9.2 Biome: a broad ecological category

A biome is a broad ecological category associated with characteristic environmental conditions and forms of life.

Terrestrial biomes are commonly recognised using climate and dominant vegetation.

Examples include tropical rainforest, desert, grassland, boreal forest and tundra.

A biome contains many local ecosystems. These ecosystems share broad features but can differ in species composition, soils and local conditions. [11]

Two points prevent confusion:

  • Similar biomes can occur in geographically separated regions.
  • Sharing a biome does not require having identical species.

Aquatic systems are also grouped into broad categories, using features such as salinity, depth, water movement and light availability.

9.3 Biosphere: the planetary perspective

The biosphere encompasses life on Earth and the portions of the atmosphere, waters and solid Earth in which life occurs.

At this level, we examine connections among living systems and other components of the planet.

Questions include how vegetation influences the carbon cycle and how global environmental changes affect life across regions.

The biosphere interacts with the atmosphere, hydrosphere and geosphere as part of the Earth system. It does not mean that every part of Earth supports life. [12]

What changes as we move between levels?

The hierarchy becomes useful when we understand what each level adds.

10.1 New collective properties become meaningful

A population has an age structure. A community has species richness. An ecosystem has patterns of nutrient storage and transfer.

These properties describe systems containing multiple components.

We cannot assign a community's species richness to one individual organism.

10.2 Interactions can produce emergent properties

An emergent property arises from the organisation and interactions of components within a system.

For example, the response of a community to a disturbance may depend on interactions among its members, rather than on the response of any one species alone.

Knowing the components is essential, but understanding their connections is also necessary.

Distinguish two ideas:

  • Aggregate property: obtained by combining measurements, such as summing the biomass of individuals.
  • Emergent behaviour: produced through interactions, such as a network response that cannot be understood by simply adding isolated responses.

Ecological research uses emergence to examine how interactions generate properties at larger scales. [13]

10.3 Effects can pass between levels

A change in an individual's survival can affect population size.

A population change can alter species interactions.

Those changes may influence ecosystem processes.

Influences also operate in the other direction. Ecosystem conditions can affect communities, populations and individuals.

The levels are connected perspectives on living systems.

10.4 The hierarchy is a framework, not a rigid size chart

An ecosystem is not defined by a minimum area. A landscape is not identified merely by being "very large."

Different studies draw boundaries differently.

The hierarchy also does not represent a progression from less evolved to more evolved life.

Space, time and the boundaries of a study

Every ecological conclusion applies within some spatial and temporal setting.

Spatial scale

A study may examine one leaf, a pond, a watershed or an entire continent.

An environmental condition can vary within a small area. The temperature experienced beneath vegetation may differ from that measured in an exposed location nearby.

Temporal scale

A study may examine minutes, seasons, decades or generations.

A fish population counted during breeding may differ from the same population counted after a period of mortality or migration.

A single observation therefore provides a snapshot.

A pattern observed at one scale should not automatically be assumed to hold at another.

A study boundary does not stop ecological exchange

Researchers may draw a boundary around a pond for measurement.

Water, organisms and materials can still cross that boundary.

The boundary helps define the study; it does not necessarily isolate the system.

BioSins reading habit

Whenever you encounter an ecological result, identify the organism or system, the area studied and the period over which measurements were made.

Two traditional approaches: autecology and synecology

These terms describe the emphasis of an ecological investigation.

ApproachMain emphasisExample
AutecologyAn individual organism or one species in relation to its environmentStudying the environmental requirements of one aquatic plant species
SynecologyGroups of species or communities in relation to one another and their shared environmentStudying how a pond community is organised

Autecology can include relationships with both living and nonliving factors. Synecology examines relationships within a community and with its common environment. [14]

Do not reduce autecology to "studying only one individual." Its focus can be a species and its ecological requirements.

Modern ecology also uses approaches such as physiological, behavioural, population, community, ecosystem and landscape ecology. These approaches often overlap.

How ecological explanations are built

Ecology combines observations, experiments and models.

Consider an observation:

Aquatic plants are more abundant in the shallower part of a pond.

Several explanations are possible. Perhaps shallow water provides more light. Perhaps the sediment differs. Perhaps herbivores feed more heavily in deeper areas.

An ecologist develops a hypothesis and a prediction.

Hypothesis: Lower light availability restricts plant growth at greater depths.

Prediction: When other relevant conditions are comparable, plants receiving less light should show reduced growth.

Experiments can manipulate light while controlling other conditions. Field observations can test whether the proposed relationship also appears in the pond.

Association and causation

If plant abundance and light availability vary together, they are associated.

That association alone does not establish that light caused the abundance pattern. Other conditions may change alongside light.

Ecological reasoning asks whether the evidence distinguishes the proposed explanation from alternatives.

Models help express these explanations, generate predictions and examine consequences. Their conclusions must be interpreted in light of their assumptions.

One pond, several ecological questions

Suppose a warm period is followed by declining fish numbers.

We can investigate this event at several levels.

QuestionMain ecological focus
How does an individual fish respond to warmer water?Organism
How does survival change in fish species X?Population
How do changes in fish abundance affect prey and competing species?Community
How do temperature, respiration, decomposition and oxygen exchanges interact?Ecosystem
Can fish move between this pond and neighbouring water bodies?Landscape

The event is the same. The questions differ.

Several mechanisms might contribute to the decline. Temperature could affect the fish directly, change oxygen conditions, influence food availability or interact with disease.

The explanation requires evidence.

How to identify the level in a question

First identify what is being explained or measured.

Then identify the system to which that response belongs.

For example:

"How does water temperature affect the survival of a population of fish species X?"

The explanatory factor is abiotic, but the response concerns a population.

Mentioning an abiotic factor does not automatically make a study ecosystem ecology.

Likewise, studying many individuals does not automatically mean population ecology. An investigation might measure the physiological responses of many individuals to answer an organismal question.

APPLY THE IDEA

Classify the question, not the keywords.

Choose the main level for each response variable. These are original learning examples.

How does water temperature affect the survival rate of fish species X in this pond?

How does removal of a predator change the number and identity of species present?

How much nitrogen moves between organisms, pond water and sediment during a season?

Common points of confusion

StatementCorrect understanding
"All fish in a pond form one population."Only if they belong to the same species; multiple fish species represent multiple populations
"A species and a population are the same."A species may contain many populations
"Population size and density are interchangeable."Size is a total; density relates that total to area or volume
"A community includes the pond's water and minerals."Those belong to the ecosystem's abiotic components
"An ecosystem must be large."Ecosystems can be studied at different sizes
"All ecosystems depend directly on sunlight."Some food webs are supported by chemosynthetic primary production
"Energy is recycled like nutrients."Energy flows and becomes dispersed; nutrient elements can cycle
"Ecology concerns only untouched natural places."It also includes human environments and managed systems
"Natural systems are permanently balanced."Ecological systems change through fluctuations, disturbance and other processes

Keep these ideas together

The essential distinctions are:

  • Organism: how an individual lives in its environment.
  • Population: how individuals of one species are organised and change.
  • Community: how different species occur together and interact.
  • Ecosystem: how a community and its physical environment function together.
  • Landscape: how spatial arrangement and connections influence ecological processes.
  • Biome: how broad environmental conditions correspond to characteristic ecological systems.
  • Biosphere: how life connects with the planet as a whole.

Across all these levels, ecology seeks explanations for patterns, interactions and processes.

The level tells us where the question is focused. The connections help us explain the answer.

The next connection: from organisation to the physical environment

We now have a framework for studying life - from an individual organism to the biosphere.

But recognising the levels does not yet explain why a particular organism can live in one place and struggle in another.

To answer that, we must examine the conditions organisms actually experience.

How does temperature influence biological activity? How does light availability change across habitats? Why do water, salinity, pH and oxygen restrict some organisms while allowing others to thrive?

These physical and chemical conditions influence individuals first, but their effects can extend through populations, communities and ecosystems.

In A2: The Physical Environment, we begin with these conditions and follow how they shape the possibilities for life.
